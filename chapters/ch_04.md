# Chapter 4 — Bayou Secrets

## PAGE 31

[ART: Brooke, Teepop, and Miss Rena pushing through the thick Spanish moss and palmetto brush at the edge of the bayou, the water mirroring a plum-and-peach sunset; Brooke holds a flashlight, while Teepop carries a bulky drone case]

The Louisiana bayou doesn’t keep secrets; it just digests them. 

If you leave a piece of iron in the swamp, the water eats it until it’s orange dust. If you leave a story in the trees, the Spanish moss wraps it up until nobody can see the shape of it. It’s an easy place to hide a lot line, and an even easier place to hide a body. But on the evening we walked into the back easement behind Lot 7, the bayou was resisting. The sunset was bleeding peach and deep plum across the still water, turning the cypress knees into crooked, black fingers.

"Watch your step, sha," Teepop murmured, holding back a wet branch of sweetgum. He was carrying a heavy, waterproof plastic case that smelled like fresh solder and marsh water. "The water table's up two inches from Tuesday. One wrong step and you're wearing the swamp."

"Instinct is a bell, Brooke," Miss Rena said from behind us, her well-pressed green overalls dark in the shadows. "But in a place like this, you look at the mud. The mud don't have a reason to lie to you."

I held my flashlight low, its beam cutting the white mist off the water. "Somebody was out here three nights ago, Rena. Wiping a door is one thing. Walking the swamp is another."

## PAGE 32

[ART: Teepop kneeling on a dry patch of marsh grass, assembling a sleek, quadcopter drone with specialized floats; Cricket watches the drone curiously, while Rena stands guard under a massive live oak]

Teepop knelt on a dry hummock of marsh grass, his fingers moving with the quick, practical efficiency of a lawyer who spent his weekends herding hardware hackers. He snapped four carbon-fiber propellers onto a drone that looked more like a mechanical water bug than a camera platform. It had massive, custom 3D-printed foam floats taped to the landing gear.

"This is one of Miller’s prototypes," Teepop explained, tapping his phone to sync the controller. "He claims it’s 'mostly waterproof' and can survive a direct impact with an alligator. I told him we’d test the first part of that claim tonight."

"Mostly waterproof," I said, a dry smirk on my face, "is my favorite kind of legal liability, Henry."

"Optimal security requires aerial reconnaissance, Brooke," Dale's voice cracked over the walkie-talkie clipped to Teepop's belt. "Director Scruggs is monitoring your telemetry from Command Post One."

"Dale," I said, leaning toward the belt. "If you don't turn that radio off, I'm going to let Sheila mulch it."

The radio went silent with a soft, offended click. 

## PAGE 33

[ART: tight shot on the drone's glowing neon-green rotors lifting off into the mist, buzzing low over the dark bayou water, casting ripples on the surface]

The drone’s rotors began to spin, a high-pitched, angry hornet buzz that cut through the evening choir of bullfrogs and crickets. It lifted off, its neon-green status lights casting twin emerald rings onto the dark, glassy water before it swept out over the treeline.

Teepop watched the screen of his phone, his face lit by the cold blue glow of the telemetry feed. "Mapping the utility easement now. The thermal sensor is picking up a consistent cold spot along the bayou edge. That usually means disturbed soil—water pooling in fresh excavations."

"A developer doesn't excavate at night, Teepop," I said, my magnifying glass in my pocket, my eyes on the dark bayou.

"No," he said, zooming in on the screen. "And they don't hide their markers in the deep tupelo. Look at the camera feed, Brooke. Ten yards east of the water tower line. There’s a reflection."

"A survey flag," Rena said, her voice dropping into that quiet, heavy register that always made the room go still. "Bright pink. Hidden under the palmetto fans."

## PAGE 34

[ART: Brooke bending down in the wet mud to inspect a wooden survey stake wrapped in hot-pink plastic ribbon, while Teepop holds his phone over it to log the GPS coordinates]

We pushed through the wet palmettos, the smell of damp earth and rotting vegetation rising up around our boots. In the middle of a muddy pocket where the cypress knees formed a tight cage sat the survey marker.

It was a fresh wooden stake, driven deep into the clay. Hot-pink survey ribbon was wrapped tight around the top, the plastic tail snapping in the bayou breeze. 

I snapped on a blue glove and leaned down, my flashlight beam holding steady on the wood. Stamped into the side of the stake, in black, indelible ink, was the corporate registration number: *ACADIAN DEV LLC — PARCEL 4B.*

"GPS logged," Teepop said, his phone clicking as he saved the coordinates. "This marker aligns exactly with the Lot 7 Yellow Line we found in Bobby's binder. Somebody's consolidating the boundary, Brooke. Slicing off five feet of the park's utility buffer zone at a time."

"It's a quiet grab," I said, my finger tracing the stamp. "Five feet here, five feet there. By the time the parish board notices, the company has legal title to the whole easement."

## PAGE 35

[ART: close shot on Miss Rena Hebert; her face is suddenly pale and frozen in shock as she stares past the survey stake at an old, half-collapsed barbed-wire fence line hidden in the cypress roots]

"No," Miss Rena said, her voice barely a whisper. 

I looked up. The forensic elder, the woman who had guided my hands through twenty years of Da Berry's worst days, was standing frozen in the wet mud. Her hand, usually so steady it could hold a scalpel without a tremor, was trembling as she pointed past the survey stake.

Hidden in the cypress roots behind the marker was an old, rusted barbed-wire fence line. It was half-collapsed, the iron wires swallowed by the bark of a massive, ancient live oak that stood like a sentinel at the swamp's edge.

Rena went rigid, her breath coming in short, shallow gasps. Her eyes were locked on a specific spot on the oak's trunk—a clean, old scar in the wood where the bark had grown back over a deep, carved cross.

"Rena?" I walked toward her, my hand reaching for her shoulder. "What is it?"

"I knew this would come back," she whispered, her voice cracking like dry wood. "I just prayed... I prayed it wouldn't come through you, Brooke."

## PAGE 36

[ART: Brooke standing beside Rena, looking at her with a concerned and highly observant expression, while Rena pulls her green overall straps tight and stares down at the mud]

Rena didn't move. She stood there under the dark live oak, her face set in a look of ancient, heavy grief. The volleyball coach, the godmother to my girls, the woman who had never flinched at a coroner's case, looked suddenly smaller in the plum bayou shadows.

"Rena, talk to me," I said, my voice quiet but firm. "You recognize this fence line. You know what's buried here."

She pulled her green overall straps tight, a familiar defense mechanism, and looked down at the mud. "This ain't just a survey, baby. It's an excavation. They're clearing the easement because they want to dredge the bayou line."

She looked at me, her eyes dark and full of a warning she didn't want to speak. "Thirty years ago, before McMurrary was coroner, before you ever snap on a glove, a girl from Eret went into this swamp and never came out. The parish ruled it a runaway. But the fence... the fence was the boundary of the search."

She turned away, her voice tightening. "Don't dig here, Brooke. Some graves are deeper than the water."

I went very still. I logged her reaction, filing it under the things folks said too fast. 

## PAGE 37

[ART: Teepop looking at his phone screen, showing a digital tax ledger showing six identical consolidated transaction rows under the name "Acadian Development LLC"]

"I've got the corporate filings," Teepop said, his voice breaking the heavy silence. He was holding his phone up, showing a digital tax ledger he’d pulled from the parish database. "Acadian Development LLC hasn't just been busy this week. Over the last six months, they've quietly purchased the quitclaim deeds for six surrounding parcels."

"Six?" I turned back to him, my detective duster rustling. "Who sold them?"

"That's the loop," Teepop said. "The sellers are listed as 'deceased' or 'unknown heirs' in the parish records. The deeds were all executed under a special parish code—Section 12, which allows the coroner's office to settle property boundaries for estate clearance."

"So McMurrary didn't just sign the plats," I said, my teeth clicking. "He facilitated the transfers. He used his authority as coroner to declare these properties abandoned or unsettled, and then signed them over to the shell company."

"It's a legal loop, sha," Teepop said. "Perfect on paper. It looks like a standard high-end developer consolidating acreage for a golf course."

## PAGE 38

[ART: Brooke looking out over the misty bayou, her turquoise necklace reflecting the green status lights of the drone hovering overhead; her face is set in a hard, dangerous smirk]

"They want us to think it's a developer," I said, my voice cool. "They want the Cove to look at the blueprints and assume it's just progress. Rich people wanting a bigger view of the swamp."

"It's a good misdirection," Teepop agreed, shutting down the drone rotors as it landed softly in its plastic case. "Standard real estate plays happen all the time. The board at Crystal's Cove would never question a forty-thousand-dollar consolidation check."

"But developers don't use coroners to sign off on boundaries, Henry," I said, a dangerous half-smirk spreading across my face. "And they don't institutional-bleach a door seam to hide a transient. This isn't a land grab for a golf course."

I looked back at the live oak tree, at the carved cross swallowed by the bark. 

"They're clearing the utility easement because they need to dredge the bayou," I said softly. "They aren't trying to build something new. They're trying to make sure something old stays buried."

Cricket let out a low, vibrating growl, her scruffy gray head tilted toward the dark water. 

## PAGE 39

[ART: Teepop using a wet rag to clean a rusted metal tag attached to a secondary concrete boundary marker near the bayou's edge; Brooke holds the flashlight over it]

"There's a second marker," Teepop called out, pointing his flashlight at a concrete block half-submerged in the duckweed near the bayou's edge.

We walked to the water’s edge. Attached to the concrete block by a rusted steel rivet was a small, stamped metal tag. It was covered in green slime and iron crust. 

Teepop reached down with a wet rag, scrubbing the metal until the caked rust gave way. The flashlight beam held steady, reflecting off the clean, stamped numbers on the steel.

*DA BERRY CORONER CASE — REGISTRY #89-0412.*

"A coroner case marker?" Teepop looked up, his face pale under his cap. "On a property boundary?"

"A boundary marker," I said, my heart starting to thud. "That double-functions as a parish case tag. McMurrary didn't just authorize this plat. He mapped the land grab directly over the old search sector."

"He used the case coordinates," Rena said from the tree. "To draw the lot lines."

## PAGE 40

[ART: close shot on the open complaint binder on the Barnett kitchen table; Brooke's magnifying glass highlights a handwritten name circled three times in red ink on an unfiled complaint form]

By the time we got back to the kitchen, the plum bayou shadows had turned into absolute, silent night. I opened the binder to the fourth section, my gloved hand turning the heavy pages under the yellow light of the kitchen pendant lamp.

There, tucked behind a parish receipt for a registered letter, sat an unfiled complaint form Bobby had written the week before he vanished.

It wasn't about potholes or palm trees. 

It was a formal Parish Zoning Board Complaint, addressed directly to the Da Berry Parish Council. And square in the middle of the form, circled three times in Bobby's thick, angry red ballpoint ink, was the name of the official who had authorized the Acadian Development utility transfer.

*Conrad Mouton.*

I went very still. Conrad Mouton wasn't just a parish zoning official.

He was Crystal Mouton's ex-husband. The man who had left her the Cove in the divorce, and the only man in Da Berry who held the keys to the parish parcel vault.

"It's a family affair," I whispered.

Cricket growled at the dark window.

She's usually right.
