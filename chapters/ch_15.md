# Chapter 15 — Coup & Resolution

## PAGE 141

[ART: wide shot of the Da Berry Civic Center hall at 11:30 AM; morning light streams through high arched windows, illuminating the crowded gallery where Cove residents sit in matching green "Crusaders" volleyball jerseys; Conrad Mouton Sr. sits at the front defense table in a charcoal suit, surrounded by lawyers]

The high arched windows of the Da Berry Civic Center let in long, dusty shafts of late-morning sunshine, cutting through the heavy air of the packed parish chamber. 

In the gallery, the community of Crystal’s Cove occupied three entire rows of wooden benches, a sea of green-and-gold "Crusaders" volleyball jerseys providing a striking contrast to the polished marble floors. Joy Lee "Sugar" McCoy sat in the center, her leopard housecoat swapped for a neat Crusaders polo, her scruffy chin held high as she clutched a brand-new tupperware of banana pudding in her lap like a weapon. Beside her, Tammy Beaumont’s giant gold hoops caught the sunlight as she tapped her long acrylic nails against the bench.

At the front defense table, Conrad Mouton Sr. sat looking relaxed, his crisp charcoal suit immaculate, a small group of high-priced corporate lawyers whispering into his ears as they reviewed their laminated plat maps. 

He didn't look at the gallery. To a man who owned half the timber lines in Acadiana, we weren't a community. We were just an administrative delay. 

But I sat at the front row next to Miss Rena, my duster folded neat over my lap, my cowboy boots planted firm. 

"Let him smile, Rena," I whispered, my warm brown eyes fixed on the dais. "The higher they build the wall, the louder it sounds when it cracks."

## PAGE 142

[ART: Conrad Mouton Sr. standing at the podium, gesturing confidently with a gold pen; the parish council board members sit behind a curved mahogany dais, their faces neutral; Brooke stands at the back microphone, duster long, her hand resting on her Western hat]

"The noon hour is fast approaching, members of the council," Conrad Mouton Sr. said, his voice carrying the deep, wealthy drawl of a man who had bought three generations of parish elections. He gestured confidently with a heavy gold pen toward the large screen behind the dais. "The statutory filing period has closed, and the Cinder Cove lot consolidation has met every administrative requirement under Section Twelve-A. It is time to call the vote and clear the path for the parish's economic future."

Behind the curved mahogany dais, the parish board members nodded, their faces neutral, their fingers hovering over their electronic voting keys. 

I stood up. 

My Western-edge hat cast a sharp shadow over my eyes as I walked down the center aisle, my duster brushing the wooden benches as I took my place at the public microphone. 

"Mr. President," I said, my voice carrying that quiet, clinical resonance that instantly stopped the whispers in the gallery. "I am Brooke Barnett, forensic coordinator for the Crystal's Cove Community. I call for an immediate administrative halt under Section Twelve-C. The Cinder Cove filing is not complete. It is based on a fraud."

## PAGE 143

[ART: dramatic medium shot of the Civic Center overhead projector; Brooke's gloved hand points to the giant projected Acadian Chemical receipt next to Clara Hebert's 1989 photo; McMurrary's signature is visible in bold red outline]

"This council cannot consolidate land that is currently subject to an active environmental homicide investigation," I said, my gloved hand pressing a button on Teepop's portable transmitter. 

On the massive overhead screens, the neat grids of Cinder Cove’s development plat disappeared, replaced instantly by a giant, high-resolution scan of the pink Acadian Chemical duplicate ledger.

The room went dead silent. 

"This is the physical purchase ledger for forty drums of concentrated hexazinone freshwater herbicide, dated May tenth, nineteen-eighty-nine," I said, my voice echoing off the marble. "Approved and signed by Dr. Scott McMurrary, the parish coroner, and Conrad Mouton Sr. It was delivered directly to the Section Twelve Tupelo Ridge drainage coordinates."

Next to the ledger, a black-and-white photograph of Clara Hebert—Miss Rena’s seventeen-year-old niece—appeared on the screen. She was smiling, a heavy vintage camera hanging from her neck, her young eyes bright with life. 

"Clara Hebert photographed these very chemical trucks dumping the drums into the ridge water," I said, my teeth clicking. "And two days later, Dr. McMurrary signed a runaway drowning certificate to bury the tox sheet. It wasn't administrative clearance. It was murder."

## PAGE 144

[ART: Bobby Boudreaux stepping through the main gallery doors in his mud-stained yellow-and-turquoise Hawaiian shirt; he is waving a stamped blue-backed document, his face bright with Cajun triumph; the board members are standing up in absolute shock]

"And as for the claim that the Lot Five easement is abandoned?" 

I turned toward the heavy brass double-doors at the back of the Civic Center hall. 

*CLACK.* 

The doors swung open, and Bobby Boudreaux stepped into the chamber, his mud-stained yellow-and-turquoise Hawaiian shirt bright as a flare under the fluorescent lights. His gray hair was wild, his boots caked in swamp clay, but his face was absolutely glowing with Cajun triumph. 

He waved a blue-backed, red-stamped Section Twelve contest document high in the air. 

"I'm not dead, I'm not a runaway, and I sure as hell don't owe Cinder Cove a single dime!" Bobby shouted, his loud voice carrying a thick bayou grin. "Bobby Boudreaux, Lot Five, Crystal's Cove! And I filed my contest at precisely midnight last night!"

Behind the curved dais, three board members stood up in absolute shock, their mouths open. Conrad Mouton Sr. went pale as a sheet, his gold pen slipping from his fingers and clattering loud against the mahogany table. 

"This is highly irregular!" Conrad Sr.’s lead attorney stammered, his papers flying. "The resident was declared missing by parish authority!"

"He was hiding from your client's shotgun, counselor," I said, my boots planted firm.

## PAGE 145

[ART: close shot on the Civic Center projector screen; it shows the video of Crystal Mouton's live-streamed confession, her bedazzled glasses glinting red under the alarm lights; the council president is staring in grim realization]

"But we don't have to take Bobby’s word for it," I said, nodding toward Teepop at the sound board. 

On the overhead screens, the ledger scan transitioned into a crystal-clear video clip. It was Crystal Mouton’s private office, the crown molding pulsing with red alarm lights. Crystal herself stood in the frame, her monogrammed clipboard shaking, her bedazzled glasses glinting under the glare. 

*"So what if we cleared a little timber ridge thirty years ago?"* Crystal’s voice echoed through the high chamber, loud and desperate. *"It was empty land! The lot consolidation is the only thing that keeps this parish from falling off the state map! Conrad Sr. came to McMurrary's office at midnight... he said the girl had to be certified as a runaway!"*

The council chamber was completely frozen, the high-priced corporate lawyers slowly gathering their briefcases and stepping back from Conrad Mouton Sr. 

The council president sat back in his leather chair, staring at the screen in grim, undeniable realization. 

"The record," I said, my duster open, my hands resting in my pockets, "is officially corrected."

## PAGE 146

[ART: the council president striking a heavy wooden gavel down on the dais; Conrad Mouton Sr. is being escorted out of the room in handcuffs by two state police officers; the Cove gallery is erupting in cheers and high-fives]

*BANG.*

The council president's heavy wooden gavel came down on the dais with a sharp, final *clack* that shook the microphone. 

"Based on the physical evidence and the registered Section Twelve contest," the president declared, his voice stern and flat, "the Cinder Cove lot consolidation petition is hereby denied. The zoning decree is permanently rescinded, and this council orders an immediate, independent state forensic audit of all parish land transfers dating back to nineteen-eighty-nine."

The gallery erupted. 

Sugar McCoy let out a loud Cajun cheer, throwing her arms around Tammy Beaumont and nearly launching her banana pudding onto the mahogany floorboards. Ricky Thibodaux pumped his safety goggles into the air, while Dale Scruggs stood rigid, saluting the dais with a proud, tactical grin. 

At the front table, two state police officers stepped forward, the metallic *click* of handcuffs sounding loud as they secured Conrad Mouton Sr.’s wrists behind his back, leading him out of the chamber under the bright morning sun. 

Miss Rena turned to me, her dark hand wrapping warm around mine, her gold band pressing steady against my palm. 

"You did it, Bug," she whispered, her eyes bright with thirty years of cleared tears. "Clara’s truth is on the books."

## PAGE 147

[ART: Bobby Boudreaux's trailer at Crystal's Cove in the golden afternoon sunshine; Dale Scruggs and Ricky Thibodaux are carefully placing a bright blue garden gnome back onto Bobby's front porch; Bobby is standing in his doorway, grinning wide]

The golden afternoon sunshine cast a warm, peach-colored haze over the mismatched trailers and plastic flamingos of Crystal's Cove. 

At Lot 5, Bobby Boudreaux’s trailer looked exactly like it always did—his faded truck parked crooked across two spaces, his television loop of classic Acadiana sports burning bright through the window screen. 

Dale Scruggs walked carefully up the wooden steps, his tactical vest adjusted neat, his gloved hands carrying the prized blue garden gnome with the absolute reverence of a bomb-squad technician. Ricky Thibodaux walked beside him, his safety goggles resting on his forehead as he adjusted the gnome's red pointed hat. 

"Perimeter security restored, Bobby," Dale said, placing the blue gnome gently back onto its concrete pedestal next to the porch railing. "The watchman is back on duty."

Bobby stood in his doorway, his Hawaiian shirt bright under the golden hour light, a massive grin breaking through his scruffy beard. 

"He looks beautiful, boys!" Bobby cheered, patting the gnome's ceramic head. "Just beautiful! Now, somebody tell me where the crawfish is, because a man don't spend a week in a marsh cabin eating old crackers without needing some real grease!"

## PAGE 148

[ART: a massive, vibrant outdoor crawfish boil at Crystal's Cove; newspaper-lined picnic tables are covered in steaming red crawfish, corn, and potatoes; Sugar McCoy is holding a giant bowl of banana pudding, laughing as she stands next to Rena]

The center yard of the Cove had been converted into a massive, vibrant community feast, the string lights overhead flickering sweet against the plum-and-peach sunset. 

Two long picnic tables were lined with fresh newspapers, piled high with steaming, bright-red crawfish, golden ears of corn, whole garlic heads, and spicy boiled potatoes. The air was thick with the rich, peppery scent of cayenne and lemon, the laughter of the neighbors carrying a warm, joyful vibration through the trees. 

Sugar McCoy stood at the end of the table, holding a giant glass bowl of her legendary banana pudding, her Crusaders polo bright. 

"Eat up, y'all!" she shouted, dishing out a massive spoonful for Miss Rena. "This pudding is load-bearing to the celebration! I put extra vanilla wafers in it just for Bobby!"

Tammy Beaumont was pouring glasses of her latest "leopard-reserve" bathtub wine, while Ricky and Dale argued heatedly about the optimal GPS coordinates for Sheila's new grass-cutting path. 

It was messy, it was loud, and it was absolutely beautiful. The found-family of Crystal's Cove—the difficult, the dramatic, the community of people nobody listened to—had stood together, and their home belonged to them again.

## PAGE 149

[ART: Brooke and Teepop standing on the Lot 1 porch, looking out at the glowing party; Teepop is wearing his fringed jacket, holding two sweet teas, and looking down at Brooke with a warm, proud smile; Brooke is resting her head on his shoulder]

I stood on the Lot 1 porch, my Western hat resting on the railing, the warm swamp breeze rustling the edges of my duster. 

Teepop walked out of the trailer, his fringed jacket soft under the string lights, holding two tall glasses of sweet tea. He handed one to me, his fingers wrapping warm around my hand, his thumb tracing a slow, steady circle against my wrist. 

"You're not scanning the exits, Bug," he said, his voice dropping into that quiet, intimate register that always made my heart settle. 

"I don't have to, Tee," I said, taking a slow sip of the tea, letting my head rest steady against his broad shoulder. "I already checked the back door."

He let out a soft, deep laugh, his arm wrapping secure around my waist. 

For the first time in my life, the Eret danger inside my chest was quiet. I’d spent my whole life believing that to keep my mother and my community safe, I had to carry every secret alone in the dark. But looking out at the glowing crawfish boil—at Rena laughing with Sugar, at Bobby high-fiving Ricky—I knew the truth. 

I wasn't alone anymore. The Bayou Bloodhound had a pack. And they were louder than any protected man.

## PAGE 150

[ART: close shot on Brooke's desk inside her trailer; the case file binder is labeled 'Cinder Cove — Closed'; Brooke is turning the last page of the unredacted parish deed registry, her eyes widening in shock as her finger traces a hidden name]

Later that night, the music had faded into the quiet hum of the bayou crickets, the string lights casting long, peaceful shadows through my trailer window. 

I sat at my kitchen counter, the heavy binder labeled *Case Files — Crystal's Cove* open before me. I slid the Acadian Chemical receipts and my mother’s letter into the final plastic sleeves, the plastic *crinkling* soft in the quiet room. 

I’d corrected the record. The case was closed. 

But as I gathered the unredacted 1989 parish deed registry to put it away, my fingers brushed the back seam of the leather cover, where a tiny, folded sheet of modern ledger paper was tucked into the pocket. 

I slid it out, my brown eyes scanning the fresh ink. 

It was a modern deed transfer, dated only three weeks ago—right before Bobby vanished. It detailed the purchase of the vast, untouched swamp parcel adjacent to our ridge. 

My eyes went wide, my finger freezing on the signature block. 

The buyer wasn't Cinder Cove Developers. And it wasn't Conrad Mouton. 

The parcel was owned by *Eret Holdings LLC.* and signed in a sharp, familiar, institutional hand that I’d spent my childhood running from: 

*Larry Boudreaux (Managing Partner)*

I looked out the window into the dark bayou, my heart catching a cold, dangerous vibration. Larry’s stroke... Larry’s twenty years in the nursing home. 

He hadn't been sleeping. He’d been planning. 

I slowly closed the binder, my knowing half-smirk returning under the dim trailer lights. 

"Season Two," I whispered, my gloves already back in my pocket. "Here we go."
