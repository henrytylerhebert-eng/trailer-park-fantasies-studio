# Chapter 12 — Night of the Fireflies

## PAGE 111

[ART: Brooke and Miss Rena standing on the stone steps of the Civic Center as the packed crowd filters out; the bright afternoon sun is harsh, but Brooke's face is cold and set with absolute defiance]

The heavy oak doors of the Da Berry Civic Center swung closed behind the departing crowd, the echoing thud sealing the noon vote like an iron gate. Cinder Cove's lawyers were down in the gravel parking lot, their high-priced charcoal shoulders shaking with quiet, victorious laughter as they loaded their leather briefcases into black town cars. Conrad Mouton had already slipped through the back council offices, safe behind his bureaucratic fortress.

"The gavel fell, Brooke," Miss Rena said, her hand resting light on my sleeve, her coaching whistle quiet against her green overalls. "Conrad signed the consolidation. Bobby’s Lot Five is gone."

"No, Rena," I said, my boots rooted to the stone steps, my leather duster catching the hot summer breeze. "Conrad signed a consolidation that rests on a forged Section Twelve preliminary certificate. A signature written on a lie is still a forgery. Under parish code, the midnight filing deadline is the absolute legal lock. If we can get the physical deed holder—Bobby himself—to sign the active, formal family contest in front of a licensed notary before twelve tonight, the parish clerk has to freeze the consolidation regardless of what Conrad's council voted."

"Exactly twelve hours," Teepop said, walking down the steps beside us, his secure laptop bag over his shoulder. 

"Twelve hours to go under the moss, Tee," I said, my eyes dark under my hat. "We're going to find Bobby."

## PAGE 112

[ART: Brooke and Teepop on the Lot 1 porch, packing a large black Pelican equipment case; the sunset sky is a deep, glowing peach; Cricket stands alert near the case, looking toward the dark treeline]

We returned to Crystal's Cove as the hot, brassy sun began to sink into the bayou, the sky bleeding a deep, glowing peach color that turned the swamp mist into a haze of gold. On the Lot 1 porch, the yellow bug light hummed its quiet hum, but tonight it was joined by the cool, digital beep of Teepop’s nonprofit drone gear.

"Bobby's voice memo said he was hiding in the old cypress camp ridge near Crowflies," Teepop said, packing a silent-propeller mapping drone into a large black Pelican case. "It's an abandoned timber tract owned by the diocese, right behind the Crowflies Catholic school volleyball courts. The canopy is so dense that standard satellite mappings can't see the ground, Brooke. But I’ve loaded a high-resolution thermal scanner from the startup trust. If Bobby is in that ridge, his heat signature will stand out like a flare."

"The swamp is thick back there, Tee," I said, checking my heavy flashlight, my blue gloves tucked into my duster. "No trails. Just tupelo knees and mud. And McMurrary’s scouts will be searching the same ridge."

Cricket trotted over, her tail giving a single, sharp *thump* against the plastic case, her alert terrier eyes fixed on the dark bayou line. 

"She’s our perimeter, Tee," I said, scratching her scruffy gray neck. "She’ll hear the boots before they reach the water."

## PAGE 113

[ART: beautiful, romantic close shot of Brooke and Teepop standing under the pink and turquoise neon string lights of the Lot 1 porch; Teepop's large hand is cupping Brooke's cheek, their eyes locked in quiet, lived-in warmth; fireflies blink in the background]

"Brooke," Teepop said, setting the Pelican case down with a soft, rubberized click. 

He stepped close, the pink and turquoise neon string lights of the porch casting a warm, beautiful glow across his broad shoulders and his sharp, academic glasses. The bayou crickets were vibrating their summer song, and the first fireflies were beginning to blink in the dark cypress limbs like a thousand tiny green stars.

"What is it, Tee?" I asked, my boots shifting on the pine planks.

He reached out, his large, warm hand cupping my cheek, his fingers brushing the long wavy hair back from my jaw. His thumb rubbed slow over my skin, his gray eyes filled with that deep, steady marriage warmth that had kept my nervous system anchored for twelve years.

"You don't have to scan every exit when I'm standing next to you, Bug," he whispered, his voice low and incredibly sweet. "I know that little girl in Eret had to watch the door. I know you carry Kat's fear in your spine. But look at me. I already checked the back door, Brooke. I charged the lights. I packed the deeds. You aren't carrying this alone anymore. We’re in the mud together, sha. Eyes open."

I let out a long, ragged breath, my forehead leaning against his chest, my gloved hand resting over his heart. 

"I love you, Henry Barnett," I whispered.

"I love you, Bug," he said. "Now let's go find our nuisance."

## PAGE 114

[ART: Brooke and Teepop hiking through the dark, misty tupelo bayou ridge; their flashlights cut bright, dusty beams through the thick Spanish moss hanging from ancient cypress trunks; Cricket leads the way]

At nine o'clock, the swamp swallowed us whole. 

The Crowflies marsh tract was a dark, primeval world of ancient cypress trunks and massive, twisted tupelo knees rising like wooden fingers from the black mud. The air was suffocatingly humid, smelling of stagnant water, decaying oak leaves, and the sweet, heavy scent of swamp lilies. The mist hung low and thick, clinging to my long leather duster as our flashlights cut bright, dusty beams through the hanging beards of Spanish moss.

"Watch your step, Tee," I whispered, my boots sinking three inches into the wet clay. "The tupelo roots are slick. If you slip here, the mud doesn't let go."

"I've got the mapping coordinates," Teepop said behind me, his phone glowing soft in the dark as he carried the heavy Pelican case. "We’re exactly four hundred yards behind the Crowflies Crusaders volleyball courts. The thermal drone is holding a silent hover at three hundred feet, scanning the ridge line ahead."

Cricket led the way, her scruffy gray body covered in wet mud, her nose active as she navigated the tupelo knees. She didn't bark—she was too smart for that—but every few feet she would stop, her ears perked toward the dark canopy, checking the wind.

This was the swamp where Clara Hebert had disappeared thirty years ago. The place where the Moutons had dumped the hexazinone drums under the cover of Larry’s Eret protection.

We were walking directly through the grave.

## PAGE 115

[ART: close shot of Teepop holding the rugged diagnostic tablet screen; the screen displays a bright orange-and-red human heat signature hiding inside the outline of a small cypress cabin deep in the green marsh mapping]

"Bug, stop," Teepop whispered, his hand catching my shoulder. 

I went still, my flashlight beam dropping to the mud. Teepop held up the rugged diagnostic tablet, the screen casting a bright green-and-black glow over our faces. 

On the digital display, the silent thermal drone’s mapping path was clear. Nestled deep inside a dense grove of tupelo trees, about fifty yards ahead, was the pixelated white outline of a small, weathered cypress cabin—an old, abandoned trapper's camp that had been decaying in the marsh since the nineteen-sixties.

But it was the center of the cabin that made my chest tighten.

Pulsing bright on the screen was a distinct, high-temperature orange-and-red human heat signature. The shape was curled tight in the corner of the structure, sitting perfectly still.

"He's in there," Teepop whispered. "The thermal signature is a match for a fifty-seven-year-old male. Active respiration. No other signatures in the sector."

"Let's move slow, Tee," I said, my gloved hand reaching into my duster pocket for my notebook and pencil. "Bobby’s terrified. If we surprise him, he’s likely to swing whatever he’s holding."

Cricket gave a single, quiet whine, her tail giving a nervous thump against my calf. 

She knew the watchman was near.

## PAGE 116

[ART: Brooke and Teepop entering the interior of the old cypress cabin; their flashlights illuminate the wood-paneled walls, revealing a startled Bobby Boudreaux clutching a rusty iron shovel; he is wearing a mud-stained, loud Hawaiian shirt]

The door of the old cypress cabin was warped by decades of moisture, creaking open with a long, metallic whine that made the swamp crickets go momentarily silent. I walked in first, my flashlight beam cutting through the damp dark of the wood-paneled interior.

"Bobby," I said, my voice low, warm, and steady. "It’s Brooke Barnett. The Bayou Bloodhound. We're here to correct the record."

In the far corner of the cabin, illuminated by my LED beam, Bobby Boudreaux was huddled against a stack of moldy burlap sacks. He was wearing his signature, incredibly loud yellow-and-turquoise Hawaiian shirt—now covered in dark bayou mud and green algae—his silver hair wild, his face covered in gray stubble. 

He was clutching a rusty, double-handled iron spade like a weapon, his chest heaving under his loud shirt, his eyes wide and bloodshot with absolute terror.

"Brooke?" he gasped, his Cajun voice high, cracked, and heavily inflected. The spade trembled in his hands, but as he recognized my duster and the turquoise cuffs on my sleeves, his shoulders gave a long, shuddering drop. "Mon Dieu, Brooke! I thought you were McMurrary’s suits! I thought they'd finally tracked me to the Crowflies ridge!"

He lowered the spade, setting the rusty blade against the floorboards with a soft, hollow *thud*, his face instantly breaking into a rugged, mud-smeared smile.

"You found my watchman, sha?" he blurted. "You got the drive?"

## PAGE 117

[ART: dramatic shot of Brooke kneeling beside Bobby; Bobby is pointing to his handwritten logs, his face intense and combative; Brooke's face is filled with a deep character turn of profound respect for this difficult man]

"We got the drive, Bobby," I said, kneeling beside him on the damp wood floor, my duster pooling around my boots. "We decrypted the soil maps and McMurrary's Lafayette Aesthetic trust transfers. We know everything."

"I knew it, Brooke!" Bobby blurted, his loud Cajun voice returning to its usual combative, neighborhood-meeting pitch. He tapped his chest with a thick, mud-stained finger. "I told Conrad! I told them the survey markers were encroaching six feet west! They thought Bobby was just a debtor who didn't want to pay his HOA dues! They thought they could run me out with five-hundred-dollar bait shop liens! But I measured the lot lines myself, sha! I measured the tupelo ridge coordinates! I knew they were stealing the ground to bury their dirty water!"

I looked at his wild silver hair, his dirty Hawaiian shirt, and the hand-written measurement notes he pulled from his pocket. 

The character turn hit me like a physical wave in my chest. 

This was the "difficult man" the entire Cove had written off. The man Crystal called a nuisance, the man Tammy accused of running from debt, the man the parish council had filed under "abandoned." 

He wasn't a nuisance. He was a brilliant, meticulous witness who had refused to let a protected bureaucracy erase his home. He had documented the truth because he knew that paper was the only shield the powerless had.

"You were right, Bobby," I said, my voice rich with a deep, profound respect. "You were right about every single stake."

## PAGE 118

[ART: Bobby Boudreaux signing the blue-backed Section Twelve deed contest document on Teepop's Pelican case; Teepop holds a glowing flashlight, his notary stamp active on the counter]

"We have to sign the active family contest, Bobby," Teepop said, setting the large black Pelican case flat between us to act as a desk. He pulled the blue-backed Section Twelve document from his bag, unrolling it under the bright beam of my flashlight. "Under parish code Section Twelve-C, only the physical deed holder can halt the Monday vote. If you sign this deed contest as the owner of Lot Five, the consolidation is legally frozen at the courthouse."

"Give me the pen, Tee," Bobby said, his hand reaching out. 

Teepop held the light steady, his notary stamp active on the plastic case. Bobby gripped the pen, his mud-stained hand steady as he signed his name—*Bobby Boudreaux*—in a thick, heavy, combative scrawl across the deed clearance line. 

Teepop instantly pressed his official notary seal onto the paper, the silver stamp clicking clean in the quiet cabin. 

"The record is sealed, Bug," Teepop said, his eyes bright behind his glasses. "We have the signed deed. We have the soil vial. We have the flash drive. It’s an airtight file. If we reach the Da Berry courthouse before the midnight clerk's lock, Conrad Mouton’s consolidation is legally dead on the books."

"Let's get to the truck," I said, my boots planted firm. 

We had the shield. Now we just had to carry it out of the swamp.

## PAGE 119

[ART: Brooke, Teepop, and Bobby stepping out of the cypress cabin into the dark swamp mist; Cricket is frozen in the grass, her gray tail flat, her hair standing on end; the swamp crickets have gone completely silent]

The creak of the cabin door was the only sound in the dark.

We stepped out into the thick, misty night, the swamp air hitting my face like a wet, warm towel. The pale sliver of moon was completely gone now, swallowed by the dense charcoal clouds, and the fog had grown so heavy that my flashlight beam was scattered into a hazy, white wall of water droplets.

But as my boots touched the wet mud of the path, I went very still.

The bayou crickets—the thousands of hums and croaks that had vibrated the marsh all night—had gone completely, terrifyingly silent. The air was dead, cold, and empty of sound, as if the swamp itself had held its breath.

Cricket was frozen in the grass beside my leg. 

Her scruffy gray body was rigid as stone, her tail flat and motionless, the hair along her spine standing straight on end. She let out a soft, vibrating growl from the very bottom of her throat—a dangerous, warning pitch I had never heard her use.

"Tee," I whispered, my hand dropping to my duster pocket, my fingers wrapping around my heavy brass flashlight. 

"I hear it," Teepop muttered behind me, his arm wrapping around Bobby's shoulder.

A heavy, muffled engine was idling in the dark mist ahead.

## PAGE 120

[ART: dramatic close shot of the black sedan with the medical plaque blocking the narrow swamp path; a tall, shadowed figure steps out into the headlights' red-and-white mist, holding a heavy, double-barreled shotgun]

Through the white wall of the fog, two bright, blinding circles of red-and-white headlights swept through the cypress limbs, casting long, skeletal shadows across the tupelo path.

The vehicle was parked crooked across the narrow swamp ridge trail, its massive tires sank deep into the mud.

It was the black sedan. The silver medical plaque on the bumper caught the headlights' glare, shining like an iron tooth.

The driver-side door swung open, the interior light casting a dim, yellow silhouette of a tall, shadowed figure. The figure stepped out into the wet clay, his boots crunching loud on the cypress twigs.

In his hands, held low and steady across his chest, was the long, dark steel outline of a heavy, double-barreled shotgun. 

The headlights cast his shadow long and terrifying across our path, his face obscured by the white mist, but the clinical, ambitious posture was unmistakable.

"Brooke," a rich, soothing baritone voice rang out through the silent swamp, its warmth completely gone, replaced by a cold, desperate finality. "I told you at the desk. Some records are sealed for a reason. You should have left the mud in the water."

The double-barrel raised, pointing straight at my chest.

The adversary had made his move.
