# Chapter 6 — The Blueprint

## PAGE 51

[ART: Brooke standing at the wooden workbench on the covered porch of Lot 1; the yellow bug light overhead casts a warm, heavy glow across the typed warning note flat on the table; Teepop leans over her shoulder with a focused, protective expression]

The yellow bug light on the Lot 1 porch hummed a low, summer-long note, buzzing like a trapped cicada. Under its warm, amber glow, the anonymous warning sat flat on the pine workbench, held down by a brass magnifying glass. The paper was clean, white, and completely ordinary—the kind of standard twenty-pound copier stock you could buy in bulk at any office supply warehouse in Da Berry. 

*Stop looking in the mud, Bloodhound.*
*Some bodies are buried for a reason.*
*Drop the case.*
*Or the next record Brooke Barnett corrects will be her own.*

"They're calling you the Bloodhound," Teepop said, his deep voice low and steady. He adjusted his glasses, his face illuminated in the golden-hour humidity. He wasn't panicked, but his jaw was set with that thick, protective line I’d seen when the kids had the croup. "They know you, Bug. They know where you park. They know how you work."

I rubbed my thumb over the brass frame of the glass. "They know I'm looking, Tee. That's not the same as knowing me."

"It's a digital print," he said, leaning closer to peer at the typeface under the magnifying glass. "Standard twelve-point Arial. No printer alignment errors, no ink bleeding. It’s a laser jet. Clean drum. Whoever printed this has access to nice equipment. The kind you find in a corporate park. Or a parish administration vault."

Cricket sat at my boots, her scruffy chin resting on my left tooled leather toe. She let out a soft, vibrating growl, her dark eyes fixed on the driveway as if the phantom writer was still lingering in the shadows of the gravel path. I knelt down, scratching her behind the ears. 

"She's right, Tee," I said, looking back at the paper. "They aren't just trying to scare us. They're trying to keep us out of the water."

## PAGE 52

[ART: Teepop unrolling a large, high-resolution architectural plat map across the workbench; the blueprints show complex lot lines and blue water-flow markings; his fringed canvas jacket catches the edge of the screen-door light]

"Well, if they wanted us to stay out of the water, they shouldn't have left the map behind," Teepop said. He reached into his canvas bag and pulled out a tightly rolled tube of heavy paper. With a wide, sweeping motion of his large hands, he unrolled a massive, high-resolution architectural blueprint across the workspace, weighting the corners down with a pair of rusty iron clean-out valves.

"What is this?" I asked, my fingers tracing the crisp, bright blue lines that mapped the southern boundary of the parish. 

"The consolidated Cinder Cove development plat," Teepop explained, tapping the corner legend. "My legal contacts at the parish environmental trust pulled the master filings an hour ago. Crystal and her development suits haven't just been mapping the neighborhood flowerbeds, Brooke. They've been drafting a quiet, total reconfiguration of the entire southern parcel."

The blueprint was incredibly detailed. It showed the neat, rectangular lot grid of Crystal's Cove on the left, but as the lines shifted to the right, toward the dark, curving channel of the bayou, the rectangles dissolved. In their place sat a massive, unified commercial zone, marked with double-line borders and official parish water-control stamps.

"Look at the lot consolidation borders," Teepop said, his lawyer voice clicking into gear. "They aren't just buying up the swamp. They're trying to clear a fifty-foot utility easement that runs directly through our park boundary."

I leaned closer, the scent of the humid cypress woods mixing with the crisp, dry smell of fresh plotter ink. "They're re-routing the water."

"No," Teepop said, his eyes dark behind his frames. "They're re-routing the law."

## PAGE 53

[ART: close shot of Brooke's blue-gloved hand tracing the red-inked lot boundaries on the plat map; the blueprint details a 50-foot easement expansion slicing directly through Lot 5, Bobby Boudreaux's lot]

I snapped on a blue nitrile glove, the latex popping sharp in the quiet night air. I let my finger follow the solid blue line of the parish utility line as it wound down from the state road. But as it reached the edge of our mobile home park, the line turned a sharp, jagged ninety degrees.

It sliced directly through the back portion of Lot 5. Bobby Boudreaux's lot.

"They're taking Bobby's land," I muttered, the pieces beginning to slide together in my head. "The back twenty feet. The exact spot where Bobby had his prize gnomes set up."

"It's not just land, Bug," Teepop said, pointing to a small, red-lined notation in the margin of the document. *'Easement Expansion 12-A. Reversionary Parish Control.'* "If the parish expands a utility easement through private property, they have to compensate the owner. But if the property is deemed abandoned, or if the title is transferred under a Section Twelve estate clearance..."

"...the easement reverts to the parish zoning board for zero dollars," I finished, my chest tightening. "Conrad Mouton's board. And then Conrad signs the consolidation over to the Cinder Cove developers."

"Exactly," Teepop said, tapping the desk. "They didn't need to buy Bobby out. They just needed him to not be here to object to the title transfer. Because if Bobby's gone, and the lot is declared an abandoned debt liability, Crystal's HOA board can sign the quitclaim. The developers get the entire bayou ridge frontage without paying a dime of commercial easement fees."

I looked at the jagged line slicing through Lot 5. 

"Bobby didn't run because he owed five hundred dollars to the bait shop," I said, the fire in my chest sparking hot and bright. "He went to ground because he realized they were stealing the ground under his feet."

## PAGE 54

[ART: Teepop leaning against the porch railing, gesturing with a highlighted legal document; his expression is a mix of intellectual excitement and deep concern under the porch's string lights]

"Here is the legal mechanism," Teepop said, pulling a yellow-highlighted photocopy of the Da Berry Parish Administrative Code from his bag. "Section Twelve: Abandoned Estate Clearance. It's an old Reconstruction-era loophole designed for clearing abandoned marshlands when there are no living heirs."

He held the document under the amber bug light, his voice dropping into that rhythmic, legal precision he used when he was dissecting a bad health insurance contract. 

"Normally, if a resident goes missing or abandons a lot, the parish has to wait seven years to declare a death in absentia before they can touch the title," Teepop explained. "But Section Twelve has a fast-track rider. If a coroner certifies a property owner as deceased with no known heirs, and the parish posts a utility easement notice on the courthouse door, the title transfers automatically in thirty days—*unless* an active, formal contest is filed by the resident or a verified family member."

"Thirty days," I said, my hand flat on the blueprint. "And Bobby has no family. He’s got a cousin in Shreveport he hasn't spoken to since nineteen-ninety-two, and that's it."

"Exactly," Teepop said. "But here’s the trick: the thirty-day clock doesn't start when the resident goes missing. It starts the moment the coroner signs the preliminary certificate of death for the estate clearance. It’s an administrative filing, Bug. It doesn't require a body. It just requires the coroner's official sign-off that the resident is presumed deceased under a parish search declaration."

"And who is the coroner who signs those certificates?" I asked, though I already knew the answer.

Teepop looked at me, his face serious. "Dr. Scott McMurrary."

## PAGE 55

[ART: Brooke comparing Bobby Boudreaux's unfiled complaint form—covered in thick red ballpoint ink—side-by-side with the official plat map; the red ink matches the layout lines perfectly]

I pulled Bobby Boudreaux's unfiled complaint binder out from the workbench drawer. The thick plastic cover was scratched, the tab labeled *'Lot 5 Boundary / Water Easement'* sticking out like a broken bone. I flipped it open, laying the handwritten pages directly beside the blue lines of the parish plat map.

Bobby’s thick, angry red ballpoint handwriting was messy, but his measurements were flawless. The man had used a physical tape measure and a level, drawing his own crude map of the lot line.

"Look at this, Tee," I said, my finger tracing Bobby's red ink. "He didn't just write down dates. He mapped the survey stakes. *'Stake three moved six feet west. Stake four hammered into the tupelo roots.'* He was tracking the encroaching easement day by day."

"He was measuring the grab in real-time," Teepop whispered, leaning down to compare the two documents. "He caught them moving the markers. If he filed this petition with the parish council, it would have triggered an automatic, independent state audit of the entire Cinder Cove consolidation project. The whole development would have been frozen for eighteen months."

"And the developers would have lost their state tax credits," I added, my eyes narrowing. "They had a thirty-day window to close the deal, and Bobby was standing in the middle of it with a yellow tape measure and a bad attitude."

I looked at the last entry in Bobby's book, dated the afternoon of the crawfish boil. 

*'They're coming tonight to sign the board clearance. I'm going to show Conrad the true lot line. If he don't listen, I'm calling the state auditor in the morning.'*

Bobby Boudreaux hadn't skipped town. He had tried to stop a train with a piece of paper, and the train had simply rolled right over him.

## PAGE 56

[ART: Teepop holding a rugged, industrial tablet screen; the screen displays a green-and-black thermal drone flight path mapping the bayou treeline behind Crystal's Cove]

"But Bobby had insurance," Teepop said, his eyes bright with a sharp, tech-savvy energy. He pulled a rugged, rubber-cased tablet from his duster pocket, tapping the screen to bring up a glowing green-and-black thermal flight log. "I ran a deep scan on the drone flight logs from Ricky's server. Ricky's backyard drone wasn't just taking scenic photos of the ducks, Brooke. It was capturing the infrared heat signatures of the back marsh during the night of the boil."

He swiped his thick thumb across the screen, zooming in on the dense, pixelated treeline behind Lot 5.

"Look at the coordinate markers," Teepop said. "Three separate survey stakes were hammered into the marsh at two in the morning, right after the crawfish boil ended. The thermal signature shows two human shapes moving along the bayou ridge, carrying a heavy metal rod. But look where they stopped."

He pointed to a blinking red dot on the digital map. The coordinates were identical to the ones Bobby had flagged in his notebook.

"They stopped right at the old carved live oak cross," I said, my heart dropping. "The boundary marker for the search sector."

"Yes," Teepop said, his voice tightening. "The drone logs prove they weren't just checking the water line. They were marking the exact coordinates of the old runaway search sector. They're planning to dredge that entire ridge, Bug. They're going to tear up the roots, scoop out the mud, and pour six inches of concrete over the entire sector for the Cinder Cove retention pond."

They weren't just building a pond. They were burying the evidence under a ton of parish-approved cement.

## PAGE 57

[ART: close shot of an old, yellowed coroner case file folder labeled 'Registry #89-0412 — Runaway (Presumed Drowning)' flat on the workbench; Brooke's gloved hand is turning the dusty pages]

I reached into the bottom drawer of my case box and pulled out the photocopy of the old parish coroner registry tag Teepop had retrieved from the Da Berry vaults: *Registry #89-0412*. 

The paper was thirty years old, its margins yellowed and brittle, smelling of damp basement archives and old vinegar. The heading was written in a cold, official typewriter font: *'Registry #89-0412 — Runaway (Presumed Drowning) — Closed.'*

"Registry eighty-nine-oh-four-twelve," I whispered, the numbers tasting like copper in my mouth. "The runaway girl from Eret. The one they said walked into the marsh and never came out."

I turned the page, my eyes scanning the brief, clinical narrative. It was only three paragraphs long—a shockingly short record for a missing teenager. No autopsy photos, no forensic dental records, no clothing descriptions. Just a single, flat statement that the search had been conducted in the southern bayou ridge sector and concluded due to lack of evidence.

"The coordinates of the search sector in the report," Teepop said, his finger pointing to the old typed grid numbers, "are a perfect match for the new Cinder Cove dredging blueprint. The developers are consolidating the lots so they can legally dredge the exact spot where that girl was supposed to have drowned."

"They're dredging the grave, Tee," I said, the truth hitting me like a cold wave. "If they dredge the ridge and pour the concrete, whatever is left of that girl—whatever evidence Scott McMurrary missed or hid thirty years ago—disappears forever under the foundation of a luxury clubhouse."

The blueprint wasn't a development plan. 

It was a cover-up with a building permit.

## PAGE 58

[ART: Miss Rena Hebert entering the Lot 1 porch, her iconic green overalls dusty at the knees; she holds a clipboard, but her eyes are fixed on the yellowed coroner tag on the workbench, her expression completely frozen]

The screen door creaked open, the rusty spring letting out a sharp, metallic whine. 

Miss Rena Hebert walked onto the porch. She was still wearing her iconic green overalls, dusty at the knees from coaching the Crowflies Crusaders through a three-hour volleyball practice, a white whistle hanging from a black cord around her neck. She carried a clipboard under her arm, but as her eyes fell on the workbench—on the unrolled blueprint, the warning note, and the yellowed coroner registry file—she went completely still.

Her boots stopped dead on the pine planks. The clipboard slipped from her hand, clattering against the floorboards, but she didn't even look down to retrieve it.

Her eyes were fixed on the number at the top of the folder: *#89-0412*.

"Rena?" I said, stepping toward her. "What is it?"

The fierce, classy Southern Black woman who had coached three generations of Da Berry girls, who had stood between me and Larry's threats when I was a teenager, looked suddenly frail. Her hand reached out, her fingers trembling slightly as she touched the edge of the old paper. The gold band on her finger, worn thin by decades of work, caught the amber light of the bug bulb.

"Where..." Rena's voice was a whisper, dry and hollow as a winter leaf. "Where did you get this registry number, Brooke?"

"Teepop pulled it from the Da Berry archives," I said. "It's the runaway case from thirty years ago. The sector they're trying to dredge."

Rena looked up, her dark eyes filled with a deep, ancient pain that went far beyond the boundaries of a zoning dispute. 

"That child wasn't a runaway, Brooke," she said.

## PAGE 59

[ART: Rena standing tall under the amber light, her face hard and steady now as she looks at Brooke; her hand is resting flat on the old coroner report, her green overalls a stark contrast to the blue plat map]

"Rena, what are you saying?" I asked, my voice dropping into my clinical, quiet register. 

Rena stood tall, her posture straightening as she reclaimed her usual fierce, quiet authority. She placed her hand flat over the old coroner report, her dark fingers covering the words *'Presumed Drowning'* as if she could erase the lie with her bare palm.

"I’m saying the parish lied, baby," Rena said, her voice dropping into that low, cutting register that always made the volleyball gym go dead silent. "They called her a runaway because runaways don't require an active investigation. They called her a runaway because it was easier than looking at the men who were standing on the levee that night."

She looked at me, her eyes drilling into mine with a terrifying intensity. "You're chasing this land grab, Brooke. You're chasing Bobby’s lot lines and Crystal's little HOA checks. But this isn't about money anymore. It never was. These people—these suits from Da Berry—they will burn this whole park to the ground before they let you read that file."

"I know the danger, Rena," I said, my boots planted firm on the pine planks. "They left a note on my truck. They threatened my family."

"Then you drop it!" Rena snapped, her voice cracking with a rare, sudden flash of panic. "You take your girls, you take Teepop, and you get out of this cove. This isn't Eret, Brooke. You don't have to carry every broken thing your mother couldn't fix. You don't have to correct every bad report Larry's friends signed."

I looked at her, and for the first time in my life, I didn't see my mentor. I saw a survivor. And it only made me more certain.

"No, Rena," I said, my voice cold and steady as steel. "Larry’s friends got away because nobody wrote it down. Bobby wrote it down. And I'm not going to let them erase the ink."

## PAGE 60

[ART: dramatic close shot on Rena's face under the harsh porch light; her lips are tight, her eyes dark with unshed tears as she delivers the final, shocking revelation]

Rena closed her eyes, a long, heavy breath rattling through her chest. When she opened them, the anger was gone, replaced by a quiet, devastating sorrow.

"Brooke," she said, her voice barely louder than the hum of the bug light. "That girl who vanished thirty years ago... her name was Clara. Clara Hebert. She was my niece."

The porch went completely silent. The hum of the bug light seemed to vanish, replaced by the heavy, suffocating weight of the humid Louisiana night. Teepop stepped forward, his hand resting on my shoulder, his large fingers squeezing once in silent shock.

"Your niece?" I whispered.

"She was seventeen, Brooke," Rena said, a single tear cutting a silver path through the dust on her cheek. "She was bright, she was beautiful, and she saw something she shouldn't have seen out on the Da Berry parish line. When she didn't come home, I went to the sheriff. I went to the coroner. I begged them to search the ridge. But they told me I was being dramatic. They told me she’d just run off to New Orleans with a boy."

She tapped the yellowed paper with her finger, her gold band clicking against the wood.

"They signed the certificate, Brooke. They signed the runaway report to keep the search party out of the tupelo ridge. And the man who signed it—the young deputy coroner who certified that there was no evidence of foul play, the man who got his medical school tuition paid by the parish development fund three months later..."

Rena looked up, her eyes dark as the bayou water.

"...was Dr. Scott McMurrary."
