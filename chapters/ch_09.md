# Chapter 9 — High Stakes

## PAGE 81

[ART: Brooke standing in the damp, mist-covered driveway of Lot 1 at dawn; the morning light is a cold, pale gray; her hand is peeling back a bright orange, parish-stamped tow-warning sticker stuck flat onto her truck's driver-side window]

The dawn on Saturday morning arrived not with a golden glow, but with a cold, pale gray mist that hung heavy over the gravel paths. The damp air smelled of wet pine, diesel exhaust, and marsh rot. I walked out to the driveway with Cricket trotting at my side, my boots damp from the morning dew, but as I reached the truck, my feet stopped dead in the gravel.

Slapped flat onto the driver-side window was a bright, screaming-orange parish citation sticker. The gum was fresh, holding the paper tight against the glass.

*NOTICE OF INTENT TO TOW — NON-COMPLIANT VEHICLE.*
*ZONING CODE SECTION 8-A: VEHICLE ABANDONMENT AND DRAINAGE OBSTRUCTION.*
*AUTHORIZED BY THE DA BERRY PARISH SHERIFF'S DEPARTMENT.*

The signature at the bottom was a thick, messy scrawl, stamped with the official parish seal. It was an administrative warning, the kind of cheap intimidation protected men used when they wanted to clear the roads without having to answer questions. 

"They're starting the sweep, girl," I whispered, kneeling to scratch Cricket's scruffy chin. 

She let out a low, vibrating growl, her dark eyes fixed on the orange paper as if she could smell the bureaucratic ink herself. My truck wasn't obstructing anything. It had been parked in the exact same spot on the Lot 1 grass for twelve years. 

This wasn't a zoning issue. 

It was a boundary marker. They were telling me that the ground under my wheels was no longer my own.

## PAGE 82

[ART: Teepop standing at the Lot 1 screen door, his expression serious as he holds his phone out toward Brooke; the light behind him is warm but the kitchen counter is cluttered with open files]

The screen door creaked open, and Teepop stepped onto the porch. He was wearing his fringed canvas jacket over athletic shorts, but he wasn't holding his usual morning mug of chicory coffee. In his large hand, his phone was active, the screen displaying a red-and-white bank notification window.

"Brooke," he said, his deep voice carrying a hard, flat vibration that made me go quiet. "They didn't just target the truck."

I walked up the steps, my hand still holding the orange sticker I’d peeled from the window. "What did they do, Tee?"

"The stay-at-home nonprofit startup account," he said, turning the screen so I could read the bold, digital print. *'ACCOUNT STATUS: TEMPORARY ADMINISTRATIVE HOLD. REASON: REQUISITION FOR AUDIT clearance under PARISH ZONING CASE 26-09.'* "It's a complete freeze, Bug. Crescent Valley Clearing filed a petition with the parish court at five yesterday afternoon, claiming our ecosystem operator funds were tied to 'zoning obstruction assets' under Lot Five."

"They're freezing our livelihoods, Tee," I said, my chest tightening as the reality of the grab hit. "They aren't just trying to scare us out of the marsh. They're trying to starve us out of the Cove."

"They're using the standard compliance lever," Teepop said, his eyes dark behind his frames. "They make it too expensive to fight, so we have to settle the quitclaim. They're closing the loops."

## PAGE 83

[ART: close shot on Teepop's phone screen showing a digital parish council countdown timer; the clock reads '47:59:12' with the heading 'Cinder Cove Lot Consolidation Vote']

"And they've set the clock," Teepop said, tapping the phone screen to bring up the official Da Berry Parish Council agenda page. 

At the top of the screen, a red digital countdown timer was pulsing slow. 

*CINDER COVE LOT CONSOLIDATION CLEARANCE VOTE.*
*TIME REMAINING: 47 hours, 59 minutes, 12 seconds.*
*VOTE CLOSES: Monday, 12:00 PM.*

"They fast-tracked the council vote," Teepop explained, his voice tight. "Conrad Mouton used an emergency parish drainage waiver to bypass the thirty-day estate contest window. If the council votes to approve the consolidation at noon on Monday, the entire back marsh is legally declared a state utility easement. The Cinder Cove developers get their building permits, the concrete trucks roll, and Bobby Boudreaux’s Lot Five is absorbed under Section Twelve for zero dollars."

"Exactly forty-eight hours," I muttered, my mind tracking the coordinates under the water tower. 

"Forty-eight hours to prove the consolidation is built on a forgery," Teepop said. "Forty-eight hours to find Bobby's cache before they pour the concrete over the pump house foundation. If we don't have the physical evidence—the soil samples, the freshwater tox panel—by Monday morning, the parish record is sealed. The grab becomes legal history."

"The clock is running, Tee," I said, my boots planted firm. "We're going to have to dig in the dark."

## PAGE 84

[ART: Miss Rena Hebert sitting at the Lot 1 Formica kitchen counter; she is wearing her iconic green overalls, her hands cradling a Tupperware container of Joy Lee 'Sugar' McCoy's famous warm banana pudding; her face is lined with ancient sorrow]

The screen door creaked again, and Miss Rena Hebert entered the kitchen. 

She was still wearing her well-pressed green overalls, but her volleyball coach clipboard was gone. In her hands, she cradled a warm Tupperware container, the sweet, rich scent of Sugar McCoy's famous baked banana pudding drifting through the humid air of the trailer. It was a classic Cove peace offering—baked with extra vanilla wafers and a double layer of toasted meringue—but Rena’s eyes were heavy with a deep, ancient sorrow that no kitchen comfort could heal.

She set the Tupperware down on the Formica counter with a soft, plastic click.

"Sugar baked it this morning," Rena said, her voice a low, dry whisper. "She said the Barnett house smelled like trouble, and trouble always needs sugar. But I didn't come for the pudding, Brooke."

She sat on the chrome-legged stool, her dark hands flat on the yellow laminate counter. 

"I came to talk about Albert," she said, her gold band catching the pale kitchen light. "I saw the way you looked at me last night, baby. I saw the way you looked at that courthouse signature. You think my Albert was one of them. You think he helped Larry bury my niece."

"Rena," I said, stepping toward her, my hand reaching out to rest on her green-overalled shoulder. "I don't think Albert was a bad man. But his name is on the waiver."

"Albert Hebert," Rena said, a single tear cutting a path through the dust on her cheek, "was a man who loved me more than his own life, Brooke. And that was his undoing."

## PAGE 85

[ART: Brooke holding Miss Rena's hands across the counter; the light through the kitchen window is growing brighter, casting a warm, red-gold hue over their fingers; Rena's face is resolved and steady now]

"Larry Boudreaux knew Albert was the search liaison," Rena said, her dark fingers squeezing mine across the counter. "And Larry knew that Albert had a cousin in Eret who was running unregistered marsh shrimp boats. It was a silly, small-time parish infraction, Brooke. But Larry told Albert that if he didn't sign that waiver—if the search recovery team kept digging in the tupelo ridge and found what Clara had seen—he would put me in a Da Berry parish cell and dismantle my Crusaders youth league before the sun went down."

I went very still, my mind flashing back to the dark Eret kitchen, to Larry's badge shining in the dim light as he threatened my mother Kat. 

"Blackmail," I whispered.

"Larry was a protected man, Brooke," Rena said, her voice hardening into a wall of absolute, quiet authority. "He had the sheriff's ear, he had the coroner's signatures, and he had the keys to the county jail. Albert signed that waiver to keep me safe. He carried that shame in his chest until the day his heart stopped. He didn't sign it for the Moutons, baby. He signed it for me."

She looked up, her dark eyes clear and resolved as she reached out to touch my bee tattoo.

"You've got your mother Kat's big, loving heart, Brooke. And you've got Dr. McMurrary's clinical eye. But you've got your own steel spine, sha. Albert couldn't fight them back then. But you? You've got the tools. You've got the truth. You go get that record."

"I will, Rena," I said. "For Clara. And for Albert."

## PAGE 86

[ART: Teepop, Brooke, and Ricky Thibodaux gathered around the Lot 1 workbench; Ricky is holding a small, specialized seismic-scanner hammer with a glowing blue digital display screen]

"We have to go in tonight," Teepop said, unrolling a detailed diagram of the Crystal's Cove pump house. "The pump house sits directly under the rusted water tower. It's the only spot in the Cove that is completely shaded by the structure twenty-four hours a day. Bobby's notebook was clear: *'the watchman stands where the light don't shine.'* He buried the cache under the pump house floor. But the foundation is solid concrete poured in nineteen-seventy-two."

"Which is why we use the seismic surveyor widget!" Ricky Thibodaux whispered excitedly, holding up a small, specialized hammer with a glowing blue digital display screen. "I built this for a local marsh-gas exploration startup. You tap the concrete with the silicone-padded head, and the widget sends a high-frequency acoustic pulse down into the sub-structure. If there’s a hollow cavity—like a buried plastic garden gnome holding a watertight cache—the screen displays a red density spike!"

"A quiet scan," I said, my gloved hand tracing the pump house diagram. "Dale has agreed to redirect his security cameras for twenty minutes at midnight. But we have to be fast. If the developers have their own scouts in the park, they'll be watching the water tower."

"I'll handle the perimeter search, Captain," Dale said, stepping onto the porch with his tactical vest tightened. "I owe you one golf cart's worth of honesty. Nobody gets past the front gate tonight."

"Midnight," I said. "We dig."

## PAGE 87

[ART: wide shot of the rusted Crystal's Cove water tower rising against a misty, dark sky; a tiny sliver of moon is visible; two shadowed figures and a small dog creep through the wet grass toward the small concrete pump house at the base]

Midnight in Crystal's Cove was a quiet, suffocating world of swamp mist and dark water. The sky was a heavy dome of charcoal clouds, with only a tiny, pale sliver of moon cutting through the fog. The neon string lights of the trailers were mostly dark, and the only sound was the distant, rhythmic hum of the bayou crickets.

Two shadowed figures crept through the wet, knee-high grass toward the base of the old water tower. 

The tower rose like a rusted iron giant against the stars, its four massive steel legs anchored deep into the marsh mud. Nestled directly between the legs sat the small, windowless concrete pump house, its wooden door secured by a heavy parish padlock. 

Cricket trotted ahead, her scruffy gray body nearly invisible in the thick mist, her nose low to the wet ground as she scanned the gravel path for exits. 

I pulled my hood low, my leather duster catching the damp fog, my boots silent as I reached the door. Teepop stepped beside me, his large hands holding a pry bar and Ricky's seismic scanner, his face set with that practical, focused determination that always made me feel safe. 

"Dale’s cameras are looped, Bug," he whispered, his breath white in the cool damp. "We've got fifteen minutes before the system alerts the parish server."

"Lock the door behind us, Tee," I said. "Let's go under the concrete."

## PAGE 88

[ART: inside the dim, copper-scented pump house; Teepop is kneeling on the damp concrete floor, tapping it with the seismic hammer; Brooke holds a focused flashlight beam on the concrete surface; old pipes and valves frame the shot]

The interior of the pump house was a cramped, cold cavern of rusted iron, damp green moss, and copper piping. The air smelled of grease, electrical solder, and fifty years of stagnant marsh water. The main water pumps hummed a low, vibrating song that shook the concrete floor under my boots.

I clicked on my flashlight, the narrow LED beam cutting through the dark to reveal the thick, cracked concrete slab of the floor.

"Let's map it, Tee," I whispered, holding the light steady.

Teepop knelt on the damp floor, his canvas jacket rustling as he pressed the silicon head of Ricky's seismic hammer against the concrete. *Tap.* 

The blue display screen pulsed with a flat green wave. 

"Nothing here," Teepop muttered, shifting two feet to the left, near the rusted pressure tank. *Tap.* 

Another flat green wave.

He moved closer to the main intake valve—the shaded corner behind the copper pressure gauge where the light never reached. He pressed the hammer down and tapped once. *Tap.*

Instantly, the seismic display screen flashed a bright, pulsing red. The acoustic sensor registered a sharp, dramatic spike in the density mapping, showing a distinct, rectangular hollow cavity exactly six inches beneath the concrete surface.

"We found the watchman," Teepop whispered, his eyes bright behind his glasses. "He's right under the gauge."

## PAGE 89

[ART: Teepop using a small hand-drill to quiet-drill the cracked concrete, while Brooke chisels a gap open; Cricket stands alert near the heavy wooden door, her ears perked up, growling softly]

"We have to be quiet, Tee," I said, snapping on my blue nitrile gloves. "No sledgehammers. The concrete is already cracked from the water pressure."

Teepop reached into his canvas bag and pulled out a small, battery-powered rotary hand-drill. He pressed the masonry bit against the cracked seam of the concrete, drilling a series of small, quiet relief holes along the red-mapped cavity line. The soft, high-pitched *whir* of the drill was swallowed by the heavy hum of the main water pumps.

I stepped in with a steel cold-chisel and a small rubber mallet. With precise, practiced blows, I tapped the chisel into the drilled relief holes, the concrete splitting along the seam with a series of soft, muted *cracks.* 

Suddenly, Cricket went rigid. 

She stood alert by the heavy wooden door, her scruffy ears perked up, her scruffy chin tilted toward the screen. She let out a soft, vibrating growl, her tail flat and motionless.

"Tee," I whispered, my hand freezing on the mallet. "Headlights."

Through the cracks in the wooden door, a sweep of bright, sweeping white light cut through the mist of the driveway. A vehicle was moving slow along the southern bayou road, its engine muffled but heavy.

"Keep drilling, Tee," I said, my voice dropping into my clinical, quiet register. "We don't leave without the cache."

## PAGE 90

[ART: dramatic close shot of Brooke's blue-gloved hand reaching deep into a chiseled concrete cavity; her fingers have gripped the dirty, weathered blue plastic base of the garden gnome; the flash drive and soil vial are visible inside the hollow base]

Teepop gave one final, heavy heave on the pry bar. With a wet, grinding sound, a massive chunk of nineteen-seventy-two concrete popped free, revealing a dark, dirt-filled hollow cavity beneath the slab.

I dropped my flashlight, knelt into the mud, and reached my blue-gloved hand deep into the dark gap.

My fingers brushed against something hard, cold, and distinctly plastic. 

I gripped the dirty, weathered surface, my muscles straining as I pulled it upward through the narrow chiseled opening. 

Out of the mud emerged the missing blue garden gnome. It was dirty, smeared with damp marsh clay, but its plastic face was still smiling that quirky, trailer-chic smile. The bottom base of the gnome had been fitted with a heavy, threaded black watertight plumbing seal.

I grabbed the seal, twisting it with a sharp, latex-pop of my gloves. 

The seal gave way with a soft hiss of trapped dry air. 

I tipped the gnome upside down over the workbench light. Out of the hollow plastic base slid two objects:

The first was a heavy, rugged black USB flash drive, stamped with an encrypted legal security logo. 

The second was a small, sealed glass vial containing a thick, gray-brown soil sample, labeled in Bobby's neat, red ballpoint handwriting: *'Tupelo Ridge Search Sector — Hexazinone Sample #1.'*

We had the physical proof. 

"We got it, Tee," I whispered, holding the cache in my hand. 

Cricket gave a single, sharp wag of her tail. 

The record was now ours to correct.
