# Chapter 2 — False Leads

## PAGE 11

[ART: Brooke at her kitchen table looking over Bobby's detailed complaint binder, turquoise rings catching the morning light, while Cricket sits by her boots watching her intensely; warm, dusty-rose bayou morning light filtering through the window]

The Cove wanted Bobby Boudreaux to be a runner. It was a comfortable story, like an old shoe that didn’t pinch. A man who owes six months of back lot rent, three tabs at Tammy’s bathtub winery, and fifty dollars to Dale Scruggs for "unauthorized perimeter protection" is a man who has an excellent reason to look at the highway and keep walking. Skipping town is a neighborhood tradition in Acadiana, sha, as old as the crawfish boil and twice as easy to clean up after.

But Bobby Boudreaux loved his grievances too much to leave them behind.

I flipped a tabbed page in the heavy binder. A crank doesn’t number his complaints. A runner doesn’t leave his prize gardens unwatered, his Crocs sitting by the steps, or his jar of emergency cash—sixty-two dollars in crumpled singles—nestled right under the kitchen sink. Runners take their money, and they sure as hell take their boots. Bobby had walked out of Lot 5 in his socks, or he hadn’t walked out at all.

"He didn't run, Cricket," I murmured, rubbing my forearm right over the bee tattoo. "A man who runs doesn't leave the fan blowing on his favorite gnawing chair."

Cricket gave a soft, high whine and nudged my boot. She knew.

## PAGE 12

[ART: Dale Scruggs standing beside his customized golf cart in a tactical vest over a floral Hawaiian shirt, pointing proudly at a grainy, black-and-white trail-cam printout he's holding up; Brooke watching him with a dry half-smirk]

"I'm telling you, Brooke, it's a transient operation." Dale Scruggs leaned against the hood of his golf cart like he was presenting a case to a grand jury. He adjusted the Oakleys parked on his forehead, his face set in a look of profound, tactical significance. "Perimeter Cam Nine caught a subject at 0300 hours. Moving stealthy. Tactical duster, low-profile cap. Drifter, guaranteed."

He slapped a piece of paper onto the cart’s windshield. It was a printout from one of his fourteen trail cams—the one he called 'Lincoln'—and it was so grainy it looked like a close-up of a charcoal briquette in a blizzard. 

"Dale," I said, leaning in to squint at the gray smear. "That looks like a raccoon carrying a plastic bag."

"That is a highly suspicious silhouette, Brooke," he corrected, tapping the paper with a stubby, acrylic-opening finger. "I've logged it in the Cove Security Registry. Under 'Threat Level: Orange-Peach.' We gotta alert Crystal. Optics are compromised."

"You logged a blurry trash bag under orange-peach?" 

"Optimal security requires absolute vigilance," he said, chest puffing out under the tactical nylon. "The Cove expects no less from the Director."

## PAGE 13

[ART: Tammy Beaumont leaning over the railing of her trailer porch in a leopard-print lounge robe and massive gold hoops, gesturing with a long, package-opening fingernail toward the bayou; golden-hour peach shadows stretching across the dirt]

"Dale couldn't secure a sandwich from a bold seagull, honey," Tammy Beaumont called out, her voice dripping with bathtub-wine sweetness. She was leaning over her porch railing, a leopard-print lounge robe draped over her shoulders and giant gold hoops catching the golden light. She took a slow sip of something pink from a plastic cup, watching us with those flat, knowing eyes that had seen every transaction in this park since the first trailer leveled off in '72.

"Tammy," I said, walking toward her steps. "You saw Bobby at the boil on Tuesday night. Did he look like a man about to skip?"

"Bobby looked like Bobby," she said, resting her chin on her package-opening acrylics. "Loud shirt, louder opinions. Complaining that the crawfish were too small and the beer wasn't cold enough. But he wasn't looking at the highway, Brooke. He was looking at the parish records office."

She leaned in closer, the gold hoops swinging. "He told me he finally had the parcel numbers that would 'tighten the noose' on whoever was trying to buy the back acres. He didn't run from his debts, sha. He was running toward a payout. Or a fight."

## PAGE 14

[ART: Henry "Teepop" Barnett at the kitchen counter surrounded by lunchboxes and school planners, squinting at a sleek laptop while a small speaker plays highly distorted local voices; Brooke looking over his shoulder]

By noon, I was back in our kitchen. Teepop was at the counter, surrounded by three half-packed lunchboxes and a stack of school planners, squinting at his laptop screen. He had a startup founder's "cutting-edge AI transcription app" running, trying to clean up the porch-cam audio Dale had copied for us. The speaker gave a static hiss, then a robotic, digitized voice spoke.

"Subject Thibodaux reports... autonomous tea box... wandered into the boo-rocks..." Teepop read off the screen, then looked up at me with a slow, dry smile. "I think the AI is having a minor stroke with the Acadiana dialect, Brooke. It translated 'Thibodaux' to 'Tea Box' and 'Boudreaux' to 'Boo-Rocks'."

"And 'crawfish'?" I asked, leaning over his shoulder.

"It keeps transcribing it as 'crawling fish' or 'communist plot,'" he laughed, kissing the side of my face as I leaned in. "But look here. I did get a clean pull on the parish transaction logs for the night of the boil. There was a title transfer filed digitally at 11:42 p.m. Exactly when Bobby was supposedly running down the interstate."

"A title transfer?" I went still. "On whose lot?"

"That's the trick," Teepop said, his hand covering mine. "The system says the authorization key belonged to Lot 5. But the IP address came from the parish coroner's office in Da Berry."

## PAGE 15

[ART: Miss Rena Hebert in her well-pressed green overalls, sitting on a high kitchen stool and holding a scene photograph close to her face; her expression is grave and sharp under the kitchen pendant light]

"People clean what they're ashamed of, baby." 

Miss Rena Hebert sat on our high kitchen stool, her well-pressed green overalls neat and her quiet, heavy authority filling the room the way it always did. She was holding one of the scene photos I’d snapped of Bobby's doorway, her sharp eyes scanning the frame under the kitchen light. 

"Rena," I said, pouring her a cup of chicory coffee. "The doorframe smelled like bleach under the lemon spray. Dale thinks Bobby was just a clean freak before he ran."

"Bobby Boudreaux didn't wash his coffee cup for three weeks in a row, Brooke. You know that," Rena said, setting the photo down with a soft, decisive tap of her finger. "A slob don't start scrubbing door seams with institutional bleach at midnight. Somebody was trying to get rid of a biological transfer. Cells. Hair. Saliva."

She looked at me, her eyes grave and steady. "They cleaned the entry because they wanted you to look at the mess inside and call it a runner. They staged the table, but they forgot that a real home has a rhythm. You don't straighten the cushion in a chair you just leaped out of to skip town."

## PAGE 16

[ART: close shot on Brooke's gloved hands holding the three-ring binder open, her brass magnifying glass hovering over the torn margin where a tab has been neatly sliced away with a razor]

I took the three-ring binder back out and opened it on the kitchen table. Under the magnifying glass, the margin of the plastic dividers showed a neat, straight scar. 

"Look here," I said, gesturing to Rena and Teepop. 

One of the rainbow-colored tabs was missing. It hadn't been torn out in a hurry; it had been sliced away with a razor blade, clean and flat, leaving only a tiny sliver of blue plastic stuck in the metal ring. The page that followed it was gone too. The rings were closed tight, but the margin paper was slightly frayed where the page had been pulled through the snaps.

"Somebody went into that trailer after Bobby was gone," I said, my heart starting to thud against my ribs. "They had the key, and they knew exactly what they were looking for. They took the page that mattered."

"But they were in a hurry," Rena observed, leaning over my shoulder. "They left the rest of the book because they thought we'd just laugh it off as Bobby's crazy complaints."

"They didn't know you, sha," Teepop said softly. "They thought the Cove would just let it go."

## PAGE 17

[ART: Teepop using his laptop to display a digital map of the parish, highlighting a specific red parcel box, while Brooke holds her magnifying glass to the screen; Cricket alert and watching from the floor]

"Let's trace the transaction," Teepop said, his fingers flying across the keyboard. "If the digital key for Lot 5 was used to file that title transfer at 11:42 p.m. from Da Berry, we need to know what parcel it actually transferred."

He brought up a digital parish plat map. A red highlight box began to blink on the screen, mapping the back lot lines of Crystal's Cove where the bayou treeline met the utility easement.

"This is the night-of-the-boil transaction receipt," Teepop explained, pointing to the metadata. "It’s a quitclaim deed. Bobby supposedly signed over all rights to the utility buffer zone behind Lot 5. But look at the timestamp on the digital signature."

I leaned in, my magnifying glass hovering over the screen. "11:42 p.m. On Tuesday."

"Right. Bobby was at the crawfish boil until midnight," I said, my eyes widening. "He was arguing with Dale about the beer at 11:45. Half the park saw him. He couldn't have been signing a digital deed from a coroner's computer in Da Berry."

"Exactly," Teepop said. "The signature is a forgery. The tech didn't solve it, Brooke—it just proved the lie."

## PAGE 18

[ART: Dale Scruggs looking embarrassed as Brooke holds up a trail-cam printout showing Ricky's lawnmower "Sheila" dragging a trash bag through the bushes at night, while Ricky Thibodaux stands awkwardly behind them in safety goggles]

"Threat Level: Orange-Peach has been resolved, Dale," I said, holding up the trail-cam printout in the middle of the Cove's dirt driveway.

Dale Scruggs cleared his throat, his tactical vest looking slightly deflated. Beside him, Ricky Thibodaux stood awkwardly, his safety goggles pushed up on his forehead and his defunct-startup tee looking dusty.

"It was a highly tactical mistake, Brooke," Dale muttered, looking away.

"Sheila's autonomous navigation module has a small glitch," Ricky explained, gesturing toward the green mower idling sideways in the hibiscus bushes behind them. "She got confused by the string lights and wandered into the back lot. She was dragging the trash bag because the bag-grabber arm got stuck on the recycle bin."

"So your 'drifter' in a tactical duster," I said to Dale, a dry smile spreading across my face, "was Ricky's lawnmower dragging a bag of plastic cups?"

"Optimal vigilance," Dale sniffed, adjusting his Oakleys. "Requires checking all targets. Even the mechanical ones."

Cricket gave a short, sharp bark, wagging her tail. She definitely thought the mower was guilty of being ridiculous.

## PAGE 19

[ART: Teepop standing in the kitchen doorway holding a fresh mug of coffee, looking at Brooke with a warm, equal gaze of support; she is wearing her duster, ready to head back out]

"You're going back to the back lots, aren't you?" Teepop asked, standing in the kitchen doorway. He was holding a fresh mug of coffee, watching me with that warm, equal gaze that had kept my feet on the ground for eleven years.

"Dale's drifter was a lawnmower, Henry," I said, snapping my duster collar into place. "But the title transfer and the missing tab are real. Somebody went to a lot of trouble to make Bobby disappear and steal his records."

"I pulled the parish records on the utility buffer zone," he said, walking over to hand me the keys to the truck. "It's registered under a shell company called 'Acadian Development LLC.' The registered agent is based out of Da Berry. I'm checking the corporate filings now."

He leaned in and kissed my forehead, his hand lingering on my shoulder. "You don't have to scan every exit when I'm standing next to you, sha. But I know you will. I already checked the back exit of the utility lot. It's clear, but watch the swamp side. Ground's soft."

"Thanks, Teepop," I said, holding his hand. "Keep the girls safe."

"Always," he smiled.

## PAGE 20

[ART: close shot on the open binder under Brooke's magnifying glass; the pen indentations on the next tab page are visible in the raking light, clearly showing the handwritten parish parcel number]

I went to the door, but something made me stop and look back at the binder. I snapped on a fresh blue glove and walked back to the kitchen table, tilting the magnifying glass under the raking yellow light of the pendant lamp.

The tab was sliced away, yes. But a pen leaves a trail.

When Bobby had written the tab label on the page he took, he’d used his usual heavy-handed ballpoint pen. He’d pressed down hard—hard enough to leave a deep, blind indentation on the margin paper of the *next* divider page behind it, like a ghost script in the cardboard.

I adjusted the angle of the glass. The shadows in the indentations sharpened, forming letters and numbers.

"Parish Plat Consolidate," I read aloud. "Lot 7."

I went very still. Lot 7 wasn't Bobby's trailer. Lot 7 belonged to Tammy Beaumont’s bathtub-wine operation.

I looked out the window toward Tammy's trailer, where the golden-hour peach light was turning into deep, plum bayou shadows. 

The record had been corrected on Page 11, but the mystery was just getting started.
