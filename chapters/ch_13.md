# Chapter 13 — Flashbacks & Revelations

## PAGE 121

[ART: Brooke standing in the freezing, thick swamp mist; the blinding headlights of McMurrary's black sedan cast long, skeletal cypress shadows; Dr. Scott McMurrary stands ten feet away, pointing a heavy, double-barreled shotgun straight at her chest]

The swamp mist was a cold, white wall that swallowed the light, scattering the black sedan's headlights into a heavy, glowing fog. The air was dead and completely silent, the bayou crickets frozen in the dark. Ten feet away, Dr. Scott McMurrary stood crooked in the wet clay, his charcoal suit damp, his silver hair glittering with moisture.

The double-barrel of the shotgun was a dark, twin circle pointing straight at the center of my duster.

"Brooke," he said, his rich, soothing baritone carrying a flat, desperate vibration through the trees. "I don't want to do this, sha. But some files are closed for a reason. You always did have a problem leaving sealed cases sealed."

Beside me, Teepop stepped forward, his arm tightening protective over Bobby Boudreaux’s shoulder, his phone active in his gloved fingers. Cricket stood rigid in the mud at my boots, a low, vibrating growl rumbling deep in her scruffy chest, her tail flat.

I didn't step back. I didn't flinch. I just let my hands rest loose in my duster pockets, my boots planted firm in the Acadiana mud. 

"The gun is a sloppy tool, Scott," I said, my voice dropping into that quiet, clinical register. "You're a surgeon. You know a clean cut is always better than a mess."

## PAGE 122

[ART: Brooke taking a slow, steady step forward in the mist, her face calm and clinical under the white glare; McMurrary's hands are visible, trembling slightly on the shotgun grip]

"You can't shoot three people in the middle of a parish search sector and call it a runaway drowning, Scott," I said, taking a slow, deliberate step forward in the wet grass. "The sheriff’s loop is gone. Larry is twenty years in a nursing home, and Conrad Mouton is a middle-man who already cracked. If you pull those triggers, the thermal drone at three hundred feet has already uploaded the logs to Teepop’s nonprofit cloud. The Cinder Cove suits won't cover for a double homicide. They'll drop you the second the state auditor asks for the coordinates."

McMurrary’s silver-framed glasses reflected the white headlights, but I could see his fingers—impeccable, clinical fingers that had performed a thousand surgical cuts—beginning to tremble slightly on the cold steel grip.

"You don't have the proof, Brooke," he muttered, his voice dropping into a desperate, frantic register. "You have stubs. Old files. Nuisance complaints from a debtor."

"We decrypted the blue watchman, Scott," I said, my voice steady as steel. "We have the Cayman transfers matching every lot consolidation. We have the unredacted soil logs proving Acadian Timber poisoned the tupelo ridge with hexazinone. And we have the raw laboratory tox sheets proving Clara Hebert was poisoned before she ever touched the canal."

McMurrary’s barrel dipped, just a hair-line fraction, his clinical armor showing a massive, final crack.

## PAGE 123

[ART: close shot on McMurrary's face in the headlights' red mist; his polished, professional mask has completely dropped, revealing a raw, desperate fear; the swamp mist clings to his silver hair]

"She... she photographed the drums, Brooke," McMurrary whispered, the polished, professional coroner mask dropping away completely to reveal the raw, desperate fear of a man who had spent thirty years running from his own shadow. "Clara. She was seventeen. She was Miss Rena's girl, always running through the back marsh with her camera. She took three photos of Conrad Sr.’s timber trucks dumping the hexazinone drums into the ridge water."

The mist clung to his face like sweat, the red glare of his taillights casting a bloody hue over his silver hair.

"Acadian Timber had a million-dollar parish land consolidation clearance riding on that ridge being declared 'unpopulated swamp,'" McMurrary muttered, his shotgun dropping until the barrels pointed at the wet clay. "If she showed those photos to the state council, the Moutons would have lost everything. Conrad Sr. came to my office at midnight. He said the girl had to be certified as a runaway. He said if I signed the drowning cert—if I buried the tox sheet—he would pay my Tulane medical tuition and guarantee my coroner appointment for life."

"So you poisoned the record," I said, the fire in my chest turning cold.

"I signed the paper, Brooke!" he gasped. "It was one signature! One little administrative clearance to keep the peace!"

## PAGE 124

[ART: Brooke standing tall, her eyes dark with fury as she remembers Kat's forced signature; McMurrary is gesturing defensively with his empty hand, the shotgun held low]

"And my mother, Scott?" I asked, my voice dropping into a dangerous, whisper-quiet registry. "Kat Barnett. Why did you make a nineteen-year-old nursing intern sign the assistant's block?"

McMurrary let out a dry, rattling laugh, his empty hand gesturing defensively in the mist. 

"Larry," he muttered. "Your stepfather. He was a raging alcoholic who had the keys to the Eret PD vault. He’d helped Conrad Sr. move the body from the ridge to the canal. But Larry wanted insurance. He knew I was getting the medical tuition, and he wanted his own piece of the pipeline. So I told Larry to bring Kat into the vault. We knew she was terrified of him, Brooke. We knew she would sign whatever Larry stood over. If we put her signature on the assistant block, we knew you—the Bayou Bloodhound, the girl who spent her whole life protecting her mother—would never, ever re-open the file. Because if you corrected the record, you’d destroy Kat's life."

"A perfect blackmail loop," I said, my teeth clicking. "You used my mother's terror to build your wall."

"It kept us safe for thirty years, Brooke!" McMurrary cried. "It kept you out of the mud!"

"Larry's protection," I said, my boots planted firm, "is dead. And my mother's record is mine."

## PAGE 125

[ART: Miss Rena Hebert stepping out of the thick swamp mist behind Brooke; her green overalls are stained with dark bayou water, and her coaching whistle hangs silver under the lights; her face is resolved and steady]

"And what about my Albert, Scott?"

Miss Rena Hebert stepped out of the thick swamp mist, her tall, regal posture cutting through the headlights' glare. Her green overalls were stained with dark bayou water at the knees, her coaching whistle hanging silver under the red mist. Her face was resolved, steady, and completely empty of fear.

McMurrary went pale as a ghost, his boots taking a stumbling step back in the wet clay. "Rena..."

"Albert co-signed that waiver because Larry threatened to put me in a Da Berry parish cell," Rena said, her voice dropping into that low, cutting register that made the entire swamp go silent. "He spent thirty years carrying that shame because he loved me. You thought you bought our silence, Scott. You thought a parish title and a charcoal suit made your lie permanent. But my Albert’s shame ends tonight. And Clara's truth belongs to the books."

She stood beside me, her dark hand wrapping around mine, her gold band pressing warm against my glove. The found-family dynamic—the mentors, the daughters, the husbands, the messy, beautiful Cove family—fused into one single, powerful block of steel.

"You always were a coward, Scott," Rena said. "Drop the gun."

## PAGE 126

[ART: dramatic close shot on Brooke's face; her eyes are bright with absolute forensic certainty; the Eret backstory trauma and her clinical nursing method have finally fused into one single moral truth]

As I stood beside Rena under the harsh headlights, the final piece of the Eret puzzle locked into my brain.

The childhood trauma of my mother's split lip, the unrecorded injustices of Larry's shift partners, the hours I’d spent in McMurrary's vault examining the dead—they weren't separate things anymore. They were the exact same method. 

The powerful call the powerless "difficult" so nobody listens. They call a staged trailer "abandonment" so nobody looks. And they call a forged Section Twelve certificate "procedural clearance" so nobody writes it down. 

My hyper-awareness wasn't fragility or magic. It was the forensic discipline taught by women who had to survive. Documentation wasn't just administrative paperwork; it was the only permanent record that protected the powerless against protected men. 

"Scott," I said, my voice cold and steady as steel. "My mother signed the lie because she was scared. I'm signing the correction because I'm not."

I held up Bobby Boudreaux’s signed deed contest, the blue-backed paper clean and sharp against the white fog.

"The file," I said, "is open."

## PAGE 127

[ART: McMurrary dropping the double-barreled shotgun into the black swamp mud; his hands are open, his head bowed in complete defeat; Teepop steps forward to kick the gun away]

Dr. Scott McMurrary looked at the blue-backed paper, then up at Teepop's glowing tablet screen, and finally into Miss Rena's dark, resolute eyes. 

The double-barrel of the shotgun slipped from his wet, trembling fingers. With a soft, heavy *splash*, the steel barrels sank deep into the black marsh mud, the camouflaged stock settling into the tupelo roots. His hands hung open at his sides, his head bowed in complete, devastating defeat.

"You always were too thorough, Brooke," he whispered, his baritone voice hollow and dry. "I trained you too well."

"No, Scott," I said, my boots planted firm. "You trained me to read the dead. Rena taught me how to fight for the living."

Teepop stepped forward with a swift, broad motion of his boot, kicking the shotgun deep into the brackish canal weeds where the marsh would swallow the steel forever. He reached out, securing McMurrary's wrists with a set of plastic industrial zip-ties from his tool bag.

"We have exactly sixty minutes to reach the courthouse, Bug," Teepop said, checking his watch. "The midnight clerk's lock is at twelve. Let's get to the truck."

"Bobby," I said, turning to the Hawaii-shirted witness. "You're driving with us."

## PAGE 128

[ART: Brooke, Teepop, Bobby, and Rena loading into the cab of Brooke's truck; Teepop is holding the signed deed contest paper; the dashboard clock glows '11:15 PM' in red digital ink]

The cab of my faded truck was packed to the doors, smelling of damp duster leather, bayou mud, and sweet banana pudding. Teepop sat in the middle, his large shoulders pressed against mine as he held the blue-backed Section Twelve deed contest tight in his lap. Behind us, Bobby Boudreaux sat on the jump seat, his mud-stained Hawaiian shirt bright under the red digital glow of the dashboard clock.

*11:15 PM.*

Miss Rena sat by the passenger door, Cricket curled tight in her lap, her scruffy chin resting on Rena’s green overall sleeve.

"We've got forty-five minutes, Brooke," Teepop said, his phone mapping the fastest route through the dark Acadiana back roads. "The Da Berry courthouse is twenty-two miles north. If we hit any parish patrol stops—if Conrad Mouton’s sheriff’s friends try to block the county line—we won't make the filing lock."

I slammed the truck into gear, the engine letting out a loud, aggressive growl that shook the steering wheel under my gloved hands.

"Larry's friends don't control the roads tonight, Tee," I said, stepping hard on the accelerator. 

The truck surged forward, its tires throwing gravel as we tore out of the swamp toward the state road.

## PAGE 129

[ART: Brooke's faded truck tearing down a wet, oak-canopied parish highway under the midnight sky; the headlights cut a long white path through the fog; the courthouse clock tower is visible in the far distance]

The parish highway was a long, black ribbon of wet asphalt cutting through the dark canopy of the live oaks. The headlights of my truck threw a long, sweeping white path through the low-hanging fog, the engine screaming at its absolute limit as I pushed the needle past eighty.

Beside me, Teepop held the phone steady, tracking our progress. 

"Ten minutes to midnight, Brooke!" he shouted over the roar of the wind. "We're entering the Da Berry parish line! The courthouse tower is coming up!"

In the far distance, rising above the dark stone roofs of the town, the neoclassical white limestone clock tower of the Da Berry Courthouse glowed pale under the cloudy midnight sky, its large black hands pointing straight at the vertical numbers.

*11:53 PM.*

I spun the wheel, the truck’s tires screaming as I tore around the courthouse square, parking crooked across two official sheriff spots without a single flinch. 

"Go! Go!" I shouted, throwing the door open.

We scrambled out of the cab, our boots crunching loud on the stone steps as we raced toward the heavy brass doors of the parish archive entrance.

## PAGE 130

[ART: Brooke standing in front of the parish clerk's desk; the clerk is stamping the blue-backed deed contest document with a massive red ink stamp that reads 'FILED — JUN 01 12:00 AM'; Brooke's face is filled with victory, but her hand is holding a yellowed sheet showing a missing page gap]

*CLICK.*

The parish clerk’s massive red ink stamp came down on the blue-backed Section Twelve contest document with a sharp, heavy, triumphant *clack.*

*FILED — JUN 01 — 12:00 AM.*

"The filing is recorded, Mrs. Barnett," the clerk said, sliding the stamped copy across the wooden desk. "The Cinder Cove lot consolidation is officially frozen pending a full, independent state forensic audit of the southern drainage coordinates. Conrad Mouton's zoning decree is suspended."

We had won. The Cove was saved. 

Bobby Boudreaux let out a loud Cajun cheer, Dale Scruggs pumped his tactical fist in the gallery, and Teepop wrapped his arms around my duster, his warm hug lifting my boots off the floorboards. 

But as I stood under the clerk’s lamp, reviewing the unredacted nineteen-eighty-nine laboratory files one last time, my eyes went dead silent.

I turned the page, my finger tracing the paper seam. The physical file was missing a crucial page—the original laboratory sheet that identified the *source* manufacturer of the hexazinone herbicide drums. Without that manufacturer record, we couldn't prove Dr. McMurrary and Cinder Cove Developers were criminally liable for murder. 

And the purchase records weren't in the courthouse.

They were locked inside Crystal's Cove—in the private, secure home files of Crystal Mouton's office.
