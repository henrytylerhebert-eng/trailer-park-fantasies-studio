# Chapter 7 — Lot Lines

## PAGE 61

[ART: Brooke sitting at the Formica kitchen counter of Lot 1, holding a steaming mug of dark-roast chicory coffee; Teepop stands opposite her, pointing at a list of shell companies spread between half-eaten pieces of toast]

The morning sun through the kitchen window of Lot 1 was thick, pale, and heavy with the promise of a ninety-degree day. The kitchen smelled of chicory coffee, toasted sourdough, and the sharp, chemical tang of the blue nitrile gloves drying on a rack by the sink. I cradled my mug, letting the dark, hot brew wake up the places that Rena's revelation had gone cold last night.

"They're called Bayou Ridge Holdings," Teepop said, sliding a sheet of printed corporation records over the counter. "Registered in Delaware. No physical office. The local mailing address is just a box at a UPS store in Da Berry. But look at the legal incorporation date, Brooke."

"Three days before Bobby Boudreaux filed his first zoning complaint about the drainage canal," I said, reading the neat columns of print. 

"Exactly," Teepop said. "And the authorized agent who signed the corporate charter is an estate management entity called 'Crescent Valley Clearing.' Which is owned by Crystal Mouton's parent holding company. They've been buying up the back acreage using a dozen different masks. They consolidated the parcel maps so they could execute the easement transfer without the park realizing it until the bulldozers showed up."

I took a slow sip, the bitter heat of the chicory settling in my chest. "It’s a perfect loop. Crystal secures the land from the inside. Conrad signs the zoning clearance from the parish side. And the developers get a clean commercial corridor."

"Except for one thing," Teepop said. "Bobby's complaint map. And Clara Hebert's file."

## PAGE 62

[ART: Brooke pulling her long leather duster from a hook by the trailer door; Cricket sits by the screen door, her ears perked up; Teepop watches Brooke pack her flashlight and notebook]

"I need that original coroner's file, Tee," I said, setting my empty mug in the sink with a sharp, ceramic click. "The digital registry entry for Clara's drowning is just a stub. Three paragraphs of bureaucratic nothing. If McMurrary signed the runaway certificate, the original physical logs will have the raw notes. The tox panel. The search coordinates."

"McMurrary’s vault is in the basement of the Da Berry Courthouse," Teepop warned, leaning against the counter. "It's not open public record, Bug. You can't just walk in there with a library card. The basement archivist has been there since nineteen-eighty-four, and she does not like tourists."

"I spent four years in that basement, Tee," I said, reaching for the hook by the screen door and pulling my long, weathered duster over my shoulders. "I know how the boxes are stacked. I know which shelves hold the cases nobody wanted to write down."

I tucked my brass magnifying glass and a fresh notebook labeled *'Things Folks Said Too Fast'* into the deep duster pockets. Cricket trotted over, her scruffy gray chin tilted up, tail giving a single, hopeful thump against the linoleum. 

"Not this time, girl," I said, kneeling to scratch her chest. "Courthouses smell like old floor wax and bad decisions. You stay here with Tee and watch the driveway."

"Be careful, Brooke," Teepop said, stepping forward to press a brief, warm kiss to my forehead. "McMurrary is polished. But polished glass is easy to crack."

"I don't want to crack him, Tee," I said, opening the screen door. "I just want to read what's behind the glass."

## PAGE 63

[ART: wide shot of Brooke's faded truck driving down a narrow, oak-canopied road toward the imposing stone facade of the Da Berry Parish Courthouse; the sky is a heavy, humid gray]

The drive from Crystal's Cove to Da Berry was a slow transition from the warm, wild green of the swamp to the cold, stone reality of parish authority. My truck’s engine hummed its usual rattling song as I drove under the heavy, low-hanging limbs of the ancient live oaks, their gray beards of Spanish moss brushing the top of my windshield.

Da Berry was a courthouse town that had grown up around its legal records. The centerpiece was the parish courthouse itself—a massive, neoclassical monument of white limestone that looked completely out of place in the humid middle of Acadiana. Its tall columns were stained with damp green mildew at the bases, and the wide concrete steps were always occupied by men in short-sleeved shirts smoking cigarettes and talking about zoning boards.

I parked my truck in the gravel lot, the heat of the engine radiating through the hood as I stepped out into the humid air. 

This was the town where Eret’s deputies sent their reports when they wanted them to disappear. The town where Larry’s friends sent the files to be stored under three inches of dust, safely out of reach of the women who lived in the trailers.

I adjusted my Western-edged hat, my boots crunching on the gravel as I walked toward the wide stone steps. 

I wasn't a nursing assistant anymore. I wasn't the scared girl from Eret. 

I was the Bayou Bloodhound. And I was here to correct the record.

## PAGE 64

[ART: Brooke entering Dr. Scott McMurrary's office; the room is modern, cool, and clinical, with high-backed leather chairs and a polished glass desk; McMurrary sits behind it in a perfectly tailored charcoal suit, looking up with a smooth, empty smile]

Dr. Scott McMurrary's office was an oasis of expensive, air-conditioned silence in the middle of the courthouse's damp decay. It smelled of eucalyptus oil, leather, and high-end hand sanitizer. The walls were lined with framed medical degrees and local civic leadership awards, all of them polished until they reflected the cool recessed lighting.

Behind a massive, clean glass desk sat Dr. McMurrary. He was in his late fifties, his silver hair perfectly trimmed, wearing a charcoal suit that looked like it had been tailored in New Orleans. He looked up as I entered, his smooth, clinical face instantly arranging itself into a warm, practiced smile that didn't reach his gray eyes.

"Brooke," he said, his voice a rich, soothing baritone that had calmed a thousand grieving families. He set down a gold fountain pen. "It's always a pleasure when one of my finest students returns to Da Berry. I heard you were living out in the Cove. How are the girls?"

"They're growing, Scott," I said, walking to the leather chair opposite him but remaining standing, my gloved hands resting on the high back. "And they're learning to notice things."

"A family trait," he smiled, leaning back. "What brings the parish's favorite investigator back to my door? I assume this isn't a social call."

"It's Registry eighty-nine-oh-four-twelve, Scott," I said. "The drowning of Clara Hebert."

## PAGE 65

[ART: close shot of McMurrary's face; his smooth smile remains, but his gold signet ring is tapping once against the glass desk; his eyes are tight and calculating]

McMurrary didn't flinch. He was too professional for that. But his warm, practiced smile went slightly rigid at the corners, and his hand moved—just a tiny, clinical adjustment of his gold signet ring against the glass desktop. A soft *tap* that echoed in the quiet room.

"That's an old file, Brooke," he said, his voice losing just a fraction of its rich warmth. "A tragic runaway case from thirty years ago. I'm surprised you even remember the registry number. It was cleared by the sheriff's department and closed before you even began your residency in my vault."

"It was closed as an accidental drowning, Scott," I said, my voice steady. "But the digital record is empty. No autopsy report. No toxicology panel. Just a signed certificate with your signature at the bottom."

McMurrary leaned forward, his hands flat on the glass, his gray eyes tight and calculating. 

"Brooke," he said, his tone shifting into that patronizing, elder-doctor rhythm I’d heard him use on difficult nurses. "You always did have a problem leaving sealed files sealed. Some cases are closed because there is nothing left to find. Clara Hebert was a runaway who had a tragic accident in the marsh. Re-opening those old wounds doesn't help the family. It just creates noise."

"It's not noise, Scott," I said, leaning down slightly, my eyes locked on his. "It's a record. And a record belongs to the truth."

## PAGE 66

[ART: Brooke's point of view: a slightly open drawer in McMurrary's desk reveals a glossy Cinder Cove Development prospectus folder; McMurrary's hand is quietly sliding the drawer shut]

As McMurrary spoke, my eyes scanned his desk with that hyper-aware nursing discipline he’d helped me train. 

His desk was mostly clear, but the top right drawer was open about two inches. Inside, partially tucked under a leather-bound medical ledger, was a glossy, dark-plum colored folder. I recognized the gold-embossed logo instantly: *Cinder Cove Estates — Phase Three.*

McMurrary noticed my gaze. With a smooth, casual motion of his forearm, he pushed the drawer shut, the wood clicking soft and clean.

"The parish has clear regulations about vault access, Brooke," he said, his voice dropping into a colder, administrative tone. "The physical files are restricted. Unless you have a formal court order, or an active police requisition, I cannot allow you to browse the historical archive. It's a matter of confidentiality."

"Scott," I said, my voice dropping into that quiet, dangerous register. "Your ex-wife's ex-husband is Conrad Mouton. He’s currently sweating through his suit because I found Bobby Boudreaux’s zoning complaints. And I know you signed the Section Twelve clearance certificate for Bobby’s lot three days ago."

McMurrary went quiet. The gold pen in his hand remained perfectly still, but the color along his jawline was slightly uneven. He wasn't the mastermind—he was too polished to risk his medical license on a simple land grab. He was just a gatekeeper who had been bought a long time ago.

"The vault," I said, "or the state auditor. Your choice."

## PAGE 67

[ART: Brooke descending a narrow, rusty spiral staircase into the dim, concrete-walled basement vault; the air is thick with dust and old paper; a single fluorescent bulb flickers overhead]

McMurrary didn't answer for a long moment. He just reached into his desk drawer, pulled out a heavy brass key on a ring, and slid it across the glass desk without a word.

"Ten minutes, Brooke," he whispered, not looking at me. "And then I lock the door."

I took the key.

The descent into the courthouse basement was a trip into the parish's forgotten history. The spiral staircase was narrow and rusty, its iron steps groaning under my boots as the cool air of McMurrary's office was replaced by the thick, choking humidity of the underground vault. 

The basement was a labyrinth of gray metal shelving, filled with thousands of cardboard archive boxes that had been warping in the Louisiana moisture for fifty years. A single fluorescent bulb flickers overhead, casting long, nervous shadows down the narrow aisles of old paper.

I walked past the rows of nineteen-nineties filings, the smell of damp cardboard and vinegar heavy in my lungs. My flashlight beam cut through the dark, scanning the hand-written labels on the boxes until it found the row marked *'Registry — Coroner — 1989.'*

My boots stopped in front of the box labeled *'Registry 89-0400 to 89-0500.'*

I reached up, my blue-gloved hands gripping the dusty cardboard, and pulled the box down from the shelf.

## PAGE 68

[ART: close shot of the physical case file folder for Registry #89-0412; Brooke's gloved hand holds a yellowed sheet showing a toxicology report with the word 'Lethal' circled in faded red pencil]

I laid the box on an old metal table under the flickering bulb, the dust rising in a small cloud. I flipped open the lid and began searching through the folders, my fingers sliding past stubs of old car accidents and industrial mishaps.

Finally, my finger stopped on a thin manila folder: *Registry #89-0412 — Hebert, Clara.*

I pulled it out, my heart hammering against my ribs. I opened the folder under the yellow light of my flashlight.

The physical file was thin—shockingly thin—but it wasn't empty. 

Tucked behind the single-page death certificate was the original, hand-written toxicology panel from the state laboratory. It had never been uploaded to the digital database. The paper was covered in faded blue ink, but at the bottom, under the chemical analysis columns, was a word circled in faded red pencil: **LETHAL**.

I leaned closer, my magnifying glass held over the chemical line. 

"Hexazinone," I muttered, the medical term clicking in my brain. "A parish-grade forestry herbicide."

It was a highly toxic chemical used by timber companies to clear hardwoods in freshwater marshes. But the official death certificate signed by McMurrary stated that Clara Hebert's body had been recovered from the southern brackish tidal canal—a saltwater channel where hexazinone was never used and could not have accumulated in those concentrations.

Clara hadn't drowned in the canal. She had been poisoned in the freshwater tupelo ridge, miles away, and her body had been dumped in the salt canal to mimic an accidental runaway drowning.

The record had been written to hide the place of death.

## PAGE 69

[ART: Brooke examining the original signature blocks on the survey plat clearance inside the folder; she points her flashlight at a signature that reads 'Conrad Mouton Sr.' and a third, smudged signature]

"They moved the body," I said to the empty, concrete room, the cold steel of the truth settling in my bones. 

I turned the page, looking at the original land plat clearance form that had been filed alongside the coroner's certificate. Because Clara had died in the search sector, the parish had executed an immediate, temporary state safety easement over the tupelo ridge, freezing the land for "environmental clearance."

At the bottom of the easement form were the three signature blocks that cleared the land for parish consolidation.

The first was Dr. Scott McMurrary’s signature—sharp, clinical, and precise.

The second was *Conrad Mouton Sr.*—Conrad’s father, the former head of the parish zoning board, written in a thick, arrogant scrawl that matched his son’s signet-ring energy. The Moutons had been preparing this land consolidation for three generations.

But the third signature block—the one that verified the "family's consent" to waive the independent state safety audit of the search coordinates—was smudged, the ink faded and smeared by water damage. Under the smudge, I could just make out the typed title: *Parish Trustee / Eret District Representative.*

I pulled my notebook out, sketching the shape of the smudged signature with my pencil, my mind tracking the dates. 

Nineteen-eighty-nine. The year Larry was still patrolling the Eret line. The year Larry’s shift partner was the parish liaison.

The Moutons hadn't just bought the coroner. They had bought the Eret police.

## PAGE 70

[ART: close shot of the final page of the search log; Brooke's flashlight illuminates a hand-written note at the bottom signed by 'Deputy L. Boudreaux' and co-signed by 'Albert Hebert, Search Liaison']

I flipped to the very last page of the folder—the raw, handwritten field log of the search team that had cleared the tupelo bayou ridge thirty years ago. The paper was lined, yellowed, and stained with dried marsh mud at the corners.

At the bottom of the log, under the final entry declaring the search officially terminated, was a short, handwritten note from the lead search captain:

*'Search suspended at 0400 hours by parish council decree. No evidence of foul play. Family search liaison agrees to terminate active recovery efforts.'*

Below the note were the two final signatures that had sealed Clara Hebert's case forever.

The first signature, written in a sloppy, aggressive hand that I would have recognized in a blackout, was: 

*Deputy L. Boudreaux — Eret PD.*

Larry. My stepfather. The man who had spent twenty years making Eret's reports disappear.

But it was the second signature—the search liaison who had supposedly agreed to stop looking for the seventeen-year-old girl—that made the flashlight tremble in my blue-gloved hand. 

Written in a elegant, looping hand that I had seen on a hundred volleyball schedules and church volleyball rosters, was:

*Albert Hebert — Search Liaison.*

Albert Hebert. Miss Rena's late husband.

The cover-up that had buried Clara Hebert thirty years ago hadn't just been executed by the parish suits. 

It had been signed by Rena’s own family.
