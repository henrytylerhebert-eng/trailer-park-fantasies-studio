# Chapter 11 — The Trial

## PAGE 101

[ART: Brooke standing at the kitchen window of Lot 1 in the dark, her phone held tight to her ear; her face is filled with a painful, quiet tension; in the background, Teepop watches from the shadows of the hallway]

The connection to Eret was a long, scratchy line of static. The phone rang four times before a soft, trembling voice answered.

"Brooke? Baby? Is everything alright?"

"Mama," I said, my voice dropping into that quiet, careful nursing register. "I need to ask you about Da Berry. Nineteen-eighty-nine. Your first shift as an intern in Scott McMurrary's vault."

There was a sharp, gasping intake of breath on the other end, followed by a long, suffocating silence. When Kat spoke again, her voice was already thick with tears, the sound of thirty years of survival-math caution finally breaking down.

"He... he stood over me, Brooke," she wept, the Eret static crackling between us. "Larry. He had a glass of whiskey in his hand, and he was standing right behind the lab desk. Dr. McMurrary had called him in. Larry told me that if I didn't sign the assistant's certification line on that autopsy—if I made a report about the chemical results—McMurrary would withdraw the Eret police benefit checks. We wouldn't have had lights, baby. We wouldn't have had food. I didn't know the girl was poisoned, Brooke! They told me the hexazinone was just runoff from the timber canal, that it was a routine administrative sign-off to clear the parish lot lines. I was so scared."

I closed my eyes, my hand gripping the phone until my knuckles went white. Larry's badge. Larry's threat.

"I know, Mama," I whispered, the fire in my chest turning to a deep, protective warmth. "You were just trying to keep us alive. I'm going to fix it."

"I'm sorry, sha," she whispered.

"Don't be, Mama," I said, my voice cold as steel. "The record is mine now."

## PAGE 102

[ART: Brooke, Teepop, and Miss Rena gathered around the Lot 1 kitchen counter; they are sorting through thick black binders and large white presentation boards covered in plat maps and scanned emails]

Sunday night in Lot 1 was a war room. 

The chrome-legged Formica counter was completely covered in thick black binders, unrolled blue plat maps, and large white presentation boards Teepop had secured from his startup ecosystem office. The air smelled of chicory coffee, raw paper, and the sweet, rich scent of Sugar McCoy's banana pudding that still sat on the shelf. 

"We have to present this as a single, airtight chain of custody," Teepop said, mounting a large corporate flow chart onto one of the presentation boards. " Bayou Ridge Holdings clear to Crescent Valley Clearing, leading straight to Dr. McMurrary's private medical trust. We have the decrypted Delaware wire transfers matching every single Section Twelve clearance Conrad signed."

"And the chemical discrepancy," Miss Rena Hebert said, her green overalls clean and sharp, her dark eyes steady. She stood beside me, her gold band reflecting the overhead kitchen bulb. "We have the laboratory tox panel proving Clara had hexazinone in her lungs when she was dumped in that salt canal. We have the signatures. Larry's. My Albert's. And Kat's."

"McMurrary thinks he’s safe because thirty years have passed," I said, my boots planted firm. "He thinks Eret’s lies are buried under thirty inches of parish dust. But a lie doesn't have a shelf life, Tee. And tomorrow morning, we're going to correct the books in public."

Cricket sat at my feet, her tail giving a single, heavy *thump* against the floorboards. 

She was ready for the fight.

## PAGE 103

[ART: wide shot of the packed Da Berry Civic Center hearing room; the walls are wood-paneled and illuminated by harsh fluorescent lights; Conrad Mouton sits at the council podium, looking smug behind a microphone; Cinder Cove's lawyers sit in a neat row]

Monday morning at the Da Berry Civic Center was a sea of parish bureaucracy and nervous tension. The hearing room was packed to the doors, the wooden benches occupied by anxious Crystal's Cove residents—Tammy in her leopard print, Dale in his tactical vest, Sugar McCoy holding a worried tissue—surrounded by a neat row of Cinder Cove's high-priced corporate zoning lawyers in charcoal suits.

At the massive oak council podium sat Conrad Mouton. He wore a polished, expensive gray suit and his gold signet ring caught the harsh fluorescent light as he tapped a gavel against a stack of official folders. His face was smooth, smug, and completely empty of concern.

"Case number forty-six-twelve," Conrad's voice boomed through the microphone, echoing off the wood-paneled walls. "The Cinder Cove Phase Three easement consolidation. The zoning board has reviewed the Section Twelve clearances for the southern marsh boundary. As there are no active, verified contest filings on record, the board is prepared to call for the final council vote to consolidate the parcels."

"Mr. Chairman," a voice rang out from the back of the room, clear and cold as iron.

The entire gallery went dead silent. 

Every head turned toward the aisle. 

I stood at the back door, my long leather duster sweeping, my deerstalker hat set firm. My tooled leather boots echoed sharp against the linoleum as I walked down the center aisle toward the presenter desk.

## PAGE 104

[ART: Brooke standing at the public microphone in front of the council podium; she is adjusting her duster, her gloved hands flat on the presenter desk; Conrad Mouton looks down from his seat with a patronizing, narrow-eyed expression]

"This consolidation," I said, my voice carrying clean through the chamber without the aid of the microphone, "is a forged record, Conrad."

A low, buzzing murmur swept through the gallery, Crystal's Cove residents leaning forward, while Cinder Cove's lawyers instantly stood up, their paper ledgers rustling like dead leaves.

"Brooke Barnett," Conrad Mouton said, his voice dropping into that flat, patronizing council rhythm. He leaned over the high desk, his signet ring tapping once against the wood. "You are a resident of a mobile home park. You have no legal standing in this parish chamber, and your administrative objections have already been evaluated and closed internally. This is a formal council hearing, not a neighborhood coffee meeting."

"I have the standing of the record, Conrad," I said, my gloved hands flat on the presenter desk, my boots planted firm. "And the record says you've been accepting twenty-thousand-dollar Cayman wire transfers from a Delaware shell trust for every Section Twelve clearance you've signed."

Conrad went pale, his narrow eyes widening as I reached into my duster pocket and pulled out a thick black binder, slapping it onto the center of the presenter desk with a loud, echoing *slam.*

"This," I said, "is the audit."

## PAGE 105

[ART: close shot of Brooke projecting a large flow chart on the Civic Center's projector screen; the chart displays the Lafyette Aesthetic Trust routing lines leading straight to Conrad Mouton's Grand Cayman bank account]

"Zoning clearance is an administrative detail, Mr. Chairman," Cinder Cove's lead lawyer shouted, gesturing frantically to the clerk. "This is unverified gossip! This resident has no formal documentation to support these slanderous accusations!"

"Then look at the bank guarantees," I said, my hand tapping the presenter controller. 

With a soft, digital click, the Civic Center's massive projector screen flashed bright. 

On the screen emerged a massive, high-contrast corporate flow chart. The lines were stark and clean, mapping the wire transfers from *Lafayette Aesthetic Trust* directly through the Delaware shell companies, leading in a straight, undeniable red path to Conrad Mouton’s Cayman bank routing numbers. Every date matched a signed lot consolidation decree on the parish books.

The gallery erupted into chaos. Sugar McCoy gasped, Dale Scruggs let out a loud, tactical whistle, and Conrad Mouton scrambled to slam his gavel down, the wooden mallet cracking loud against the podium.

"Order! Order in the chamber!" Conrad screamed, his face turning a deep, sweating red. "The clerk will shut down the projector! This is a procedural violation!"

"The procedural violation," I said, my voice cutting through the noise like a surgical blade, "is using parish zoning laws to hide a thirty-year-old murder, Conrad."

## PAGE 106

[ART: Brooke pointing to the projector screen showing the 1989 unredacted toxicology report; the word 'Hexazinone' and 'LETHAL' are highlighted in high-contrast red overlay]

"Thirty years ago," I said, my boots rooted to the floorboards as the projector screen shifted to display the unredacted nineteen-eighty-nine laboratory tox sheets, "a seventeen-year-old girl named Clara Hebert vanished from the Da Berry line. The parish coroner certified her death as an accidental drowning in a brackish canal."

I pointed a blue-gloved finger at the screen, where the chemical analysis columns were highlighted in a high-contrast red digital overlay.

"But the raw laboratory record proves she had lethal concentrations of *hexazinone* in her lungs," I explained, my clinical authority freezing the room. "A freshwater forestry herbicide. Hexazinone is never used in brackish canals. It is used in the tupelo bayou ridge—the exact ridge Cinder Cove is trying to dredge today. Clara Hebert was poisoned in the ridge because she saw the Acadian Timber company dumping the drums to clear the landowners. And she was dumped in the salt canal to hide the site of her death."

Dr. Scott McMurrary, sitting in the front row of the gallery, went completely rigid. 

His silver hair was perfectly trimmed, and his charcoal suit was impeccable, but his clinical, smooth face had drained of all color. He looked like one of his own cold vault cases, his hands flat on his knees, his eyes fixed on the screen in a silent, terrifying realization.

## PAGE 107

[ART: close shot of Dr. Scott McMurrary in the gallery, sweating under the harsh Civic Center lights; Brooke's projector screen behind him shows his young signature from 1989 side-by-side with his modern Cinder Cove signature]

"The young deputy coroner who signed that false drowning certificate," I said, my voice dropping into a quiet, relentless register, "was Dr. Scott McMurrary."

I tapped the controller again. 

On the screen emerged the side-by-side comparison of McMurrary's signatures—the young doctor's ambitious scrawl from nineteen-eighty-nine, and the modern clinical hook from the Cinder Cove easement guarantee. 

"The hand that certified the lie thirty years ago is the hand signing the developer checks today," I said, my boots planted firm. "He’s the silent partner financing Bayou Ridge Holdings. He’s using Cinder Cove's Phase Three retention pond to pour six inches of concrete over the ridge coordinates, burying the physical soil evidence of his own poisoning cover-up forever before the state auditor can drill it."

McMurrary didn't move. He adjusted his glasses, his signet ring trembling slightly, a thin sheen of sweat reflecting the fluorescent lights. 

"Mr. Chairman," Cinder Cove's lawyer stammered, his face white. "This... this is an ancient administrative anomaly! It has no bearing on the current commercial zoning consolidation! The thirty-day Section Twelve contest window for Lot Five has closed!"

"The window," a new voice boomed from the gallery, "is wide open."

## PAGE 108

[ART: Miss Rena Hebert standing beside Brooke at the presenter desk, her green overalls clean, her white volleyball whistle hanging from her neck; she is holding a formal parish contest document, looking Conrad Mouton straight in the eyes]

Miss Rena Hebert stood beside me, her posture tall and regal, her coaching whistle hanging from her neck like a silver medal of office. She laid a formal, blue-backed parish contest document onto the desk, her dark fingers flat against the paper, her gold band shining under the Civic Center lights.

"Albert Hebert co-signed the search waiver thirty years ago because Eret's deputies threatened his home," Rena said, her voice dropping into that low, cutting register that always made the volleyball gym go silent. "But I am standing here today. I am Clara Hebert’s family. And as her surviving aunt, I am filing an active, formal family contest under Section Twelve of the Da Berry Parish Code."

Conrad Mouton stared at the blue-backed document, his signet ring trembling as he looked between Rena and his ex-wife Crystal, who sat pale and silent in the suspect row.

"This..." Conrad muttered, looking toward Cinder Cove's lead lawyer. "This filing is... procedurally irregular."

"It is procedurally perfect, Conrad," I said. "Rena’s contest freezes the consolidation easement until an independent state forensic team can drill the tupelo ridge coordinates."

The room held its breath, the countdown timer on the screen pulsing slow: *00:00:45.*

## PAGE 109

[ART: Cinder Cove's lead lawyer smiling coldly as he taps a document in front of Conrad Mouton; Conrad looks up, a smug, bureaucratic smile returning to his face]

Cinder Cove's lead lawyer looked down at a sheet of paper in his hands, and suddenly, a cold, smug smile returned to his face. He stepped to the podium, whispering two sentences into Conrad Mouton’s ear before sliding a highlighted page of the parish code under the chairman's hands.

Conrad looked down at the paper, and I watched his shoulders relax, the color returning to his face as that smug, bureaucratic indifference locked back into his jawline.

"The board," Conrad Mouton said, his voice dropping back into that cold, institutional wall, "must reject the contest filing."

"On what grounds, Conrad?" I asked, my boots planted firm.

"Under Da Berry Parish Code Section Twelve-C," the lead lawyer said, turning to face me with a patronizing, narrow-eyed expression. "A family contest can only halt an active consolidation vote if the contesting relative holds the physical deed to a consolidated lot. Miss Rena Hebert is a respected parish resident, but she does not own a lot inside Crystal's Cove. She has no reversionary interest in the soil."

"And Bobby Boudreaux," Conrad Mouton added, a smug smile returning to his face, "is legally declared deceased under the preliminary coroner's certificate. Unless Mr. Boudreaux files the contest in person, the deed to Lot Five reverts automatically to the parish at noon. And the clock, Brooke..."

He pointed to the screen.

The countdown timer read: *00:00:03.*

## PAGE 110

[ART: dramatic close shot of the Civic Center clock hitting exactly 12:00 PM; Conrad Mouton raised his wooden gavel high, preparing to strike the oak block, while Cinder Cove's lawyers watch with victory in their eyes]

The red digits on the projector screen pulsed one last time before resolving into solid, glowing zeros.

*00:00:00.*

*NOON.*

"The time for filing has closed," Conrad Mouton said, his voice flat and triumphant as he raised his wooden gavel high above the oak block. "As there is no physical deed holder present in this chamber to verify the contest, the zoning board calls for the immediate vote to approve the Cinder Cove Phase Three easement consolidation."

"Mr. Chairman!" Teepop shouted from the gallery, his hand reaching for his phone as Cinder Cove's lawyers watched with victory in their cold eyes. 

The sheriff's deputy stepped forward, his hand resting on his gun belt, blocking the aisle as Conrad Mouton prepared to strike the block.

"We proceed with the vote," Conrad said, his signet ring catching the light as the gavel began its final, downward arc. "The consolidation is approved."

The wooden gavel fell.

And the Cove was gone.
