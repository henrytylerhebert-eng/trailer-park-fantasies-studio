# Chapter 3 — Trailer Tricks

## PAGE 21

[ART: Crystal Mouton standing on the aluminum steps of Lot 5, holding her bedazzled monogrammed clipboard to her chest like armor; Brooke stands in the dirt below her, looking up calmly with a steel expression]

Crystal Mouton was waiting for me on the porch of Lot 5, looking like she’d been styled by a corporate board that only met at a swamp-side outlet mall. Her velvet scrunchie was a perfect dusty rose, matching the piping on her monogrammed athleisure. She held her bedazzled clipboard against her chest like a breastplate, her heels clicking on the aluminum steps as she shifted her weight.

"Brooke, honey, bless your heart for staying on this, but we really must think about the community," she said, her voice dropping into that warm, lifestyle-brand purr she used when she wanted to raise rent or explain away a leak. "A missing-person investigation... it sends such a dreadful signal to prospective tenants. The Cove is a luxury brand, honey. Standards don't lower themselves."

"Crystal," I said, my boots planted in the damp Acadiana dirt. "Bobby Boudreaux didn't skip town. His prescription organizer is still on the counter, and someone scrubbed the door seams with bleach."

"Oh, that binder's just Bobby being Bobby," she scoffed, waving a French-manicured hand. "He filed parish complaints about the moss on the live oaks, Brooke. The man was a nuisance. He Skip-towned. It happens at properties... below a certain level. Let's let the sheriff handle it."

"The sheriff," I said, "is currently in Da Berry eating donuts, Crystal. I'm going back inside."

## PAGE 22

[ART: Brooke standing inside the dimly lit trailer, wearing blue nitrile gloves and holding a weekly pill organizer up to the window light, her face focused and analytical]

A trailer has a rhythm, same as a body. You can stage the surface, but the details will always tell on you. 

I snapped on a fresh blue glove and walked past Crystal, the screen door slapping shut behind me. Inside, the trailer was quiet, smelling of cold coffee and lemon-fresh lie. I went straight for the small bathroom cabinet. A nurse doesn’t look at the clutter; she looks at the shelf. 

There it was: a blue plastic weekly pill organizer, sitting neat beside the toothbrush holder. 

Bobby Boudreaux was fifty-seven, with a back that had been bent by forty years of diesel mechanics and a parish-certified case of rheumatoid arthritis. The Monday-through-Sunday compartments were filled with his daily methotrexate and high-dose naproxen—the red pills for morning, the yellow ones for night. Only Monday and Tuesday were empty. Wednesday's morning slot was still packed tight.

A man who runs from his debts takes his joints with him. 

He doesn’t walk down the interstate into a Louisiana humidity lock without the only medicine that keeps his hands from curling into fists. This wasn't a voluntary exit. This was a staged scene, wiped to a shine by someone who knew how a runner's house should look but didn't know what it felt like to live in a body that hurt.

## PAGE 23

[ART: Brooke bending down beside the television console in Bobby's trailer, her magnifying glass hovering over the glowing DVD player menu screen, which displays a static loop of a fireplace]

"Look at the screen, Brooke," Bernie's voice murmured from the door. 

I turned. Our oldest daughter was leaning against the wood-paneled doorframe, her quiet, sharp-eyed gaze scanning the room with the dry wit she inherited from her father and the pattern-catching eyes she got from me. Bernie didn't chase attention; she just stood still long enough to catch the details everyone else was too loud to notice.

"The TV," she said, pointing a finger at the blue flicker. "It's not on a channel. It's a DVD menu."

I bent down beside the particle-board television console. Bernie was right. The blue light flickering through the curtain gap wasn't the morning news or a parish channel. It was the static, looping menu of a "Cozy Bayou Fireplace" disc, repeating its ten-second track of crackling wood over and over.

"A man who skips town in a hurry doesn't stop to put in an ambient DVD and set it to loop, Bernie," I said, my magnifying glass catching the dust on the player tray.

"And he doesn't leave the remote control squared up exactly with the edge of the television stand," Bernie added, her dry side-eye meeting mine. "Somebody wanted the neighbors to see a blue glow and assume Bobby was just watching the game."

## PAGE 24

[ART: Dale Scruggs outside the trailer, poking a broomstick aggressively into the lattice underpinning of Bobby's trailer, while Ricky Thibodaux stands by looking worried]

Outside, the search was degenerating into camp theater. 

Dale Scruggs was currently on his knees in the dirt, poking a broomstick aggressively into the white plastic lattice underpinning of Bobby's trailer. His tactical vest was cinched tight over a floral Hawaiian shirt, and his Oakley sunglasses were pushed up on his forehead like a second pair of eyes.

"Dale," I said, walking down the steps. "Are you looking for clues or just trying to annoy the copperheads?"

"Vigilance, Brooke," he wheezed, thrusting the broomstick deeper into the dark recess. "The under-lattice is a known tactical vulnerability. Bobby could have dropped evidence through the floor vents. My cams showed high-frequency movement in this sector last week."

"Your cams showed a lawnmower, Dale," Ricky Thibodaux chimed in, shuffling his feet. He was wearing his safety goggles on his forehead, looking anxious. "And speaking of Sheila... she's gone again. I left her on 'boundary trim' at Lot 6, and she's drifted completely off the grid."

"She's probably under the trailer," Bernie said, pointing to a small, fresh tear in the plastic lattice. "The grass is mulched right up to the edge."

## PAGE 25

[ART: Ricky's autonomous lawnmower "Sheila"—a green mower with an attached mechanical grabber arm—emerging backward from under the trailer's underpinning, dragging a heavy, dirt-caked metal cylinder]

A low, rhythmic grinding sound began to hum from beneath the trailer. It sounded like an angry blender chewing through gravel. 

"Sheila!" Ricky gasped, dropping his hands.

The white plastic lattice buckled, and then the green, Roomba-taped-to-a-Husqvarna machine backed slowly out of the dark under-lattice. Her blade assembly was quiet, but her "aggression module"—an attached metal grabber arm Ricky had bolted to the chassis for "debris clearance"—was clamped tight around a heavy, dirt-caked object.

"She’s got a target," Dale yelled, drawing a plastic flashlight from his vest like it was a sidearm. "Secure the perimeter!"

Sheila ground her gears, her tires spinning in the soft Acadiana mud as she dragged the heavy object completely into the light. It was a thick, industrial-grade metal surveyor's canister, the kind parish teams used to protect master plat maps from the bayou moisture. The cylinder was covered in yellow swamp mud, but the brass lock-cap was still intact.

"What is that?" Ricky asked, adjusting his safety goggles. "I didn't program her to harvest metal."

"She didn't harvest it, Ricky," I said, snapping my blue-gloved fingers as I walked over. "She found where somebody hid it."

## PAGE 26

[ART: close shot on Brooke's gloved hands twisting the caked brass cap of the metal surveyor's canister, revealing a tightly rolled blueprint map inside]

The brass cap on the canister was stiff, caked with dry clay and swamp grit. I held the cylinder firm, my blue-gloved hands twisting the screw-top while Dale and Ricky watched with held breath. 

"Careful, Brooke," Dale whispered, looking around. "That could be toxic runoff evidence. Or a parish ordinance violation."

The cap gave way with a sharp, metallic screech. Inside, protected by a thick plastic sleeve, sat a tightly rolled blueprint. I pulled it out slow, the heavy paper uncoiling on the hood of Dale's golf cart.

It wasn't a standard park map. 

It was a highly detailed parish survey blueprint, dated exactly three weeks ago. Bright red surveyor flags had been hand-drawn over specific plots, mapping a massive boundary line that sliced straight through the back lot lines of Crystal's Cove. The title block in the corner read: *Acadian Development LLC — Bayou Consolidation Plat.*

"They're consolidating," I said, my finger tracing the red boundary line. "They aren't just buying Bobby's lot. They're trying to empty the whole eastern ridge."

"But why would Bobby have the surveyor's maps?" Ricky asked, his eyes wide behind his goggles.

"Because," Bernie said quietly, "Bobby was the one who caught them trespassing."

## PAGE 27

[ART: Brooke holding the blueprint map flat against the golf cart hood, while Bernie points to a specific lot line highlighted in yellow; Dale looks over their shoulders with a confused expression]

"Look at the yellow line, Mom," Bernie said, her finger tapping the paper. 

The plat map had a specific lot line highlighted in neon yellow marker—and it wasn't Lot 5. The yellow border enclosed Lot 7, the corner plot where Tammy Beaumont ran her tub-wine ministry. Bobby’s neat, military-style handwriting was scribbled in the margin: *CONSOLIDATE TARGET 2 — EST. VALUE $45k. TAMMY DOESN'T KNOW.*

"They're moving in on Tammy's lot next," I said, my teeth clicking together. "Bobby worked it out. He saw the survey stakes in the bayou and realized they were mapping the park's boundaries without Crystal's board knowing."

"Wait, Acadian Development?" Dale squinted at the seal. "That's the company Teepop pulled from the Da Berry filings. But who owns the LLC?"

"The registered agent is a dummy corporation," I said, my eyes scanning the blueprints. "But the survey itself has a parish seal. This wasn't an independent developer, Dale. This was a parish-approved operation. Somebody in the local government signed off on these boundaries."

"But who?" Ricky asked.

"A woman who trades secrets for favors," a voice purred behind us.

## PAGE 28

[ART: Tammy Beaumont standing by the golf cart in a tight leopard-print top and giant gold hoops, holding her pink drink cup and watching Brooke with a sharp, bargaining expression]

Tammy Beaumont had arrived, her leopard-print top bright against the plum bayou shadows and her gold hoops catching the evening light. She took a slow sip of her pink drink, watching the blueprint on the cart's hood with the cool, calculating gaze of a woman who ran the Cove's informal stock exchange.

"Tammy," I said, keeping my hand flat on the map. "Bobby's binder had a note about Lot 7. He knew they were targeting your land."

"Bobby was a busybody, honey, but he wasn't blind," Tammy said, her package-opening acrylics tapping the plastic cup. "He told me three weeks ago that some suits from Da Berry were sniffing around the utility easement behind my tubs. Offered me fifty thousand to sign a quitclaim deed. I told them my grapes needed the bayou air and to go scratch."

She leaned in closer, the smell of cheap Merlot and lemon oil drifting off her. "But they didn't stop. Bobby was keeping a record for me. That's why he had the maps. He was going to file a parish injunction on Friday."

"He was protecting you," I said.

"Bobby was protecting the Cove, Brooke. But he wanted a favor in return. And now... I think I need to trade."

## PAGE 29

[ART: close split-shot: Tammy leaning in to whisper to Brooke, her heavy makeup and gold hoops in frame, while Brooke listens intently, her bee tattoo visible on her forearm]

"What favor, Tammy?" I asked, my voice low.

"Bobby wanted me to tell him who Crystal was meeting in the back lot at night," she whispered, her heavy mascara-lined eyes narrowing. "Crystal’s been running this park like a country club, honey, but she’s broke. The board’s been auditing her books. She needs this land sale to cover what she took from the maintenance fund."

She leaned in until her gold hoop brushed my collar. "Two nights before the crawfish boil, Crystal met a man in a polished navy suit by the bayou easement. He had a Da Berry parish seal on his folder. Crystal gave him the key to Lot 5. I saw her hand it over."

"She gave away Bobby's key?" I went cold. "Before he even disappeared?"

"Yes, sha. Crystal's in it deep. She’s selling the Cove out from under us to cover her own deficit. But she isn't the boss. The man she met... he's the one driving the tractor."

"Did you get a name, Tammy?"

"No," she said, straightening up and taking a sip. "But he drove a polished black sedan with a Da Berry medical plaque on the bumper."

## PAGE 30

[ART: close shot on the blueprint surveyor seal; Brooke's magnifying glass highlights the signature at the bottom: "Dr. Scott McMurrary, Parish Coroner, Da Berry"; the dark bayou shadows closing in around the page]

My breath hitched. A medical plaque.

I pulled the surveyor's canister back toward me, my heart hammering a wild, familiar rhythm in my chest. I turned the blueprint over, my magnifying glass scanning the tiny, parish-approved seals in the lower right-hand corner of the title block.

There was the official Da Berry parish stamp. 

And right beneath the seal, written in the elegant, controlled cursive of a man who used a fountain pen like a scalpel, was the approval signature.

*Dr. Scott McMurrary, Parish Coroner.*

It was my old boss. The doctor who’d taught me how to read a wound, how to document a cause, and how a system hides its worst days behind the neat margins of official paperwork. He hadn’t just signed off on the survey—he’d authorized the Acadian Development plat.

"McMurrary," I whispered, the word cold on my tongue.

The string lights of Crystal's Cove flickered rose and plum as the Louisiana dusk finally closed in, turning the bayou into a wall of absolute shadow.

The record wasn't just dirty. The coroner had signed the warrant.
