Chapter 1 — Welcome to Crystal's Cove
PAGE 1
[ART: wide establishing shot — Crystal's Cove at dawn, mist off the bayou, string lights still burning, the sign reading "uxury Living at Affordable Prices"; trailer-chic palette, dusty rose and denim blue]

I learned early that a house can lie.

A clean kitchen can lie. A smiling man can lie. A police report can lie by never getting written at all.

My mama taught me how to love soft. Larry taught me how danger walks into a room before it knocks. Nursing taught me what pain looks like when somebody's working hard to hide it. And Miss Rena taught me that the truth deserves a clean record, written down, dated, where nobody can call it gossip later.

So when folks tell me a missing man probably just got tired of his debts and drove off into the dark — I don't nod along.

I look closer.

Because trouble doesn't always leave. Sometimes it gets moved. Sometimes it gets cleaned up with the good lemon spray. Sometimes it gets filed under nothing to see here, sha, and everybody goes back to their coffee.

I've made a whole life out of seeing anyway.

My name is Brooke Barnett. Around the parish they call me the Bayou Bloodhound, which is flattering and a little ridiculous, same as everything else out here.

And on the morning Bobby Boudreaux didn't answer his door, Crystal's Cove was about to find out the difference between drama and a case.
PAGE 2
[ART: Joy Lee "Sugar" McCoy mid-stride between trailers in a leopard housecoat, pounding a door with one fist and clutching a foil-covered dish with the other; flamingos and a golf cart in frame]

Sugar McCoy found out first, because Sugar finds out everything first. It is her ministry.

She had banana pudding in one hand — she always has banana pudding, it is load-bearing to her personality — and she was using the other hand to beat on Lot 5 like it owed her money.

"Bobby!" Bang bang bang. "Bobby Boudreaux, I know you in there hidin' from me, your TV's still goin'! Bobby!"

Nothing.

The television flickered blue through the gap in the curtains. The window unit dripped. The truck sat in the drive. And Bobby — who would argue with a stop sign, who once filed a written complaint about the humidity — did not come to the door to tell Sugar to mind her business.

That was the part that turned her stomach. Not the silence. The wrongness of the silence.

By the time she'd knocked on three more doors, the Cove was awake and assembling, the way it does, like a casserole nobody asked for. By the time somebody thought to call me, half of them had already decided what happened.

He ran. He owed everybody. He finally ran.


PAGE 3
[ART: cozy interior — the Barnett kitchen, Teepop at the counter in a patterned shirt holding a toddler's lunchbox, Brooke lacing tan cowboy boots, a scruffy gray terrier alert by the door]

My phone rang at 6:52. Teepop answered it, because Teepop answers everything before it can wake the girls.

He listened. He looked at me the way he does when the day just changed shape.

"That was Sugar," he said. "Bobby's gone. Truck's there, TV's on, door's locked from somebody's idea of the inside."

I was already reaching for my boots.

My husband is built like a brick house and dresses like a man with opinions about every shirt he owns. He is a lawyer who quit the hustle to pack lunchboxes and herd founders, and he has never once, in eleven years, told me to be careful in a tone that meant be smaller.

"You want me to come?" he asked.

"I want you to keep the girls from feeding Cricket pancakes," I said. "And pull whatever the parish has on Lot 5."

He kissed my forehead. "Gloves are in the duster. Flashlight's charged. You look dangerous. I love you."

Cricket was at the door before I was, ears up, already certain somebody out there was lying.

She's usually right. More than the sheriff, anyhow.
PAGE 4
[ART: Brooke's POV walking into the Cove — Dale Scruggs in a tactical vest over a Hawaiian shirt stringing crooked caution tape, an autonomous lawnmower idling sideways in a flowerbed, neighbors in robes]

You have to understand what Crystal's Cove thinks it is.

It thinks it's a resort. It has a "clubhouse" that is a double-wide with a disco ball. It has "amenities," which is one above-ground pool and a vending machine that only sells orange soda and regret. Everybody here believes, down to the bone, that they are fabulous — and that belief is the most genuine thing about the place. I love them for it, mostly.

Dale Scruggs had already declared jurisdiction.

He'd strung caution tape from a flamingo to a propane tank to the side mirror of his golf cart, sealing off a perimeter that included, for no reason, a child's tricycle. "Director of Perimeter Security," his vest said. He bought the title the same place he bought the tape. The internet.

"Brooke." He lifted the tape for me like a maître d'. "Glad you're here. I've secured the scene."

Behind him, Ricky Thibodaux's lawnmower — Sheila, autonomous, allegedly — had wandered into the flowerbed at Lot 5 and was patiently mulching what might've been evidence.

"Dale," I said. "Your scene's eating the flowers."
PAGE 5
[ART: Crystal Mouton in monogrammed athleisure with a bedazzled clipboard, smiling too wide; Tammy Beaumont in leopard print hanging back by a trailer, sipping something pink at 7 a.m.]

Crystal Mouton arrived the way weather arrives.

She manages the Cove, which means she manages how the Cove looks, which she considers the same job. Velvet scrunchie. Clipboard with her initials in rhinestones. A smile she keeps charged at all times in case a prospective tenant or a lawsuit shows up.

"Brooke, honey, bless you for coming, but this is nothing." She said nothing like a woman smoothing a tablecloth over a stain. "Bobby owed half this park and all of Tammy's wine money. He skipped. People skip. It happens at properties below a certain — " she caught herself — "it happens."

Off to the side, Tammy Beaumont sipped her breakfast and watched me with the flat, knowing eyes of a woman who runs a wine cellar out of her bathtub and therefore knows where a great many bodies are figuratively buried.

She didn't say he ran.

Tammy noticing what she doesn't say is its own kind of evidence. I filed it.

The chorus had already settled, though. He ran. He owed everybody. Good riddance, kind of, God love him.

I'd heard that song before. Different town. Same tune.
PAGE 6
[ART: tight on Bobby's driveway — a battered truck parked crooked across two numbered spots, and beside the steps a neat row of garden gnomes with one conspicuous empty gap in the dirt]

I let them talk and I walked the lot, because the lot was already telling on everybody.

First: the truck.

Bobby parked crooked. Everybody knew that. "That's just how Bobby parks," three people told me, helpfully, like I couldn't see. But there's lazy crooked and there's crooked on purpose, and this was the second kind — slewed hard across two spots, nose to the bayou side, blocking the sightline from Sugar's porch to his front steps.

A man who wants attention parks to be seen. Bobby parked to hide something. The angle was a decision.

Second: the gnomes.

Bobby loved those ugly little men. Twelve of them, lined up by the steps in a row so even it was almost military. I counted eleven. There was a clean divot in the dirt where the twelfth had stood — soil still dark in the hole, no weeds, recent.

Cricket put her nose to the gap and growled, low, at nothing I could see.

"Somebody took a gnome," I said.

Dale wrote it down. Dale writing it down was the least useful thing happening on that lot.
PAGE 7
[ART: Brooke on the trailer steps pulling on a blue nitrile glove, head tilted toward the door seam, the crowd behind her going quiet; warm gold light, her bee tattoo visible on her forearm]

Here is the thing about gloves.

In a place like this, in a morning like that, the gloves do something a badge never could. The second I reached into my duster and snapped one on, the whole crowd went quiet behind me, like church.

Because gloves mean it's real. Gloves mean write it down.

I leaned to the door without touching it and breathed in.

Lemon. Bleach under the lemon. The good spray, the kind you use when you mean it.

Now — people clean. I'm not a fool. But Bobby Boudreaux's trailer had a smell I knew from twenty years of other people's worst days: the smell of a room that got handled. You don't deep-clean a doorframe on your way out of town with nothing.

The TV still flickered through the curtain gap. A man who runs turns off his TV, or he doesn't, but he sure doesn't leave the truck and the lights and the box fan running like the place is just waiting on him to come back from the Piggly Wiggly.

"Crystal," I said, not turning around. "Who has the key?"

A small, expensive silence.

"...I do," she said. "I'm the manager. I have all of them."

I bet you do, I thought. I filed that too.
PAGE 8
[ART: interior threshold view — a cluttered but oddly tidy trailer; recliner with the dent of a body, a thick three-ring binder squared neatly on the kitchen table beside a cold cup of coffee]

I didn't go in. You don't go stomping through a question before you've read it.

But from the threshold, with the door swung wide and the morning pouring in, the trailer told me two contradictory things at once, and contradictions are my favorite food.

Mess everywhere — Bobby was a slob, dishes, mail, a recliner worn into the exact shape of him. Lived-in. Real.

And then: too tidy. The coffee table wiped to a shine in a house that hadn't been wiped since the first Bush. The recliner cushion straightened. A drawer not quite closed, the way a drawer sits when somebody went through it fast and didn't live there long enough to know it sticks.

Somebody had been in here being careful in a house where nobody was ever careful.

And square in the middle of the kitchen table, lined up with the edge like it had been placed for whoever came next, sat a fat three-ring binder. Bulging. Tabs sticking out the side in a rainbow.

Beside it, a cup of coffee, gone cold and skinned over.

"Oh, that." Sugar peered past my shoulder. "That's just Bobby's complainin' book, sha. That man'd file paperwork on a sunrise if it came up crooked."
PAGE 9
[ART: close on Brooke's gloved hands opening the heavy binder; the crowd half-laughing behind her; pages dense with dated tabs, handwriting, photographs, parish letterhead]

Everybody laughed, because it was funny, and because laughing is what a crowd does right before it agrees to stop looking.

"He complained about my flamingos," somebody said.

"He complained the bayou smelled like bayou," said somebody else.

"That binder," Crystal announced, in her closing-argument voice, "is just Bobby being Bobby. Lord rest him, wherever he ran off to."

I opened it.

I expected a crank's scrapbook. Loose, ranting, the angry confetti of a man with too much time and not enough listened-to.

That is not what was in my hands.

It was tabbed. Dated. Cross-referenced — see also, page 14; see also, the carbon copy clipped behind. Photographs with the time written on the white border in the same careful hand. Parish letterhead. Certified-mail receipts, the green cards stapled in rows. A man who knew nobody would believe him, so he built the kind of record that wouldn't need them to.

I have seen this exact thing before. I built one once, as a girl, in a spiral notebook, about a house I couldn't get anybody with a badge to write down.

I went very still.
PAGE 10
[ART: Brooke standing slowly with the open binder, the laughing crowd faltering as they read her face; Cricket pressed against her boot; behind them the empty gnome divot and the crooked truck, dusk-warm light]

"What is it?" Dale asked. He'd stopped writing. "Brooke? It's just his crazy book, right?"

The crowd wanted me to say yes. I could feel it. Say it's nothing, Brooke, say he ran, let us go back to our coffee and our fabulous little lives where nobody has to look at the divot in the dirt.

I turned one more page. A lot-line map. A parish parcel number circled three times, hard enough to nearly tear through. A name underlined beside it that I knew. That everybody on that lot would know.

I closed the binder slow.

"No," I said. "This isn't noise."

"Then what is it?" Crystal asked, and for the first time her smile wasn't charged all the way up.

I looked at them — at Sugar with her pudding, at Dale and his tape, at Tammy not saying anything, at Crystal holding all the keys, at the eleven gnomes and the one missing, at the crooked truck hiding a sightline, at the cold coffee somebody never finished.

"It's a man," I said, "trying to be believed."

Cricket growled at the doorway.

She's usually right.
